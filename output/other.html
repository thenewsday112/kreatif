WOVEN BAMBOO pairs with CROCHETED TEXTILES IN CHAIR SCULPTURE

 

Designed by Peiyang (Camille) Li, She Blooms in the Dark is a glowing seating sculpture that examines emotional vulnerability and protective spatial thresholds. The project pairs hand-crocheted textile craft with traditional bent-wood furniture techniques, mounting an array of individual blossoms across the open-lattice framework of an oversized, woven bamboo armchair.


SHE BLOOMS IN THE DARK: Day Time View | all images ©Kexin Liu

 

 

UV-REACTIVE YARN BLOOMS STORE DAYLIGHT TO EMIT AMBIENT GLOW

 

The sculptural seat by designer Peiyang (Camille) Li functions simultaneously as a functional chair and a semi-enclosed personal retreat, utilizing a curved, basket-like silhouette to delineate an intimate interior microclimate for solitary reflection. Each floral element is crocheted by hand using phosphorescent, UV-reactive yarn engineered to absorb daylight and emit a soft, low-intensity glow in dark environments. By forgoing high-output electric fixtures in favor of photoluminescent fibers, the textile piece activates after dark, transforming the structural bamboo envelope into an illuminated shelter where soft woven media counterbalance the rigid natural framework.

maison prouvé 58 finds a new home near saint-paul-de-vence

 

Maison Prouvé 58, a prefabricated house developed by Jean Prouvé and his son Claude in 1958, has been reconstructed near Saint-Paul-de-Vence in southern France after being saved from demolition in Alsace. The low, L-shaped residence now stands among pine and olive trees, overlooking the Mediterranean landscape. Recently reopened through Les Maisons CAB, the house is available for overnight stays (see here).

 

The building originally stood in Epfig, a village in northeastern France, where it was commissioned by local notary Édouard Degott. It remained there for more than sixty years, undergoing several alterations along the way. When the municipality acquired the property for redevelopment, plans for a new residential care facility threatened the house with demolition.

 

Belgian art collector Hubert Bonnet acquired the structure in 2021 and arranged for its dismantling the following year. Its components were transported to Nancy for restoration before being reassembled in Provence, where its aluminum facade and long stretches of glazing now face an entirely different landscape.

images © Micha Pycke

 

 

jean and claude prouvé’s experiments in prefabricated housing

 

The original commission began with Jean Prouvé, whose experiments with industrialized construction had already produced a series of lightweight, demountable buildings. Working in Paris at the time, he entrusted the project to Claude, who developed the architectural plans and oversaw construction. The house was completed in 1959, despite the 1958 date retained in its present-day name.

 

Archival research from the École Nationale Supérieure d’Architecture de Nancy reveals an eventful construction process. Delayed deliveries of facade panels and glazing slowed work on site, and Claude eventually helped assemble the window frames himself. The difficulties offer a glimpse into the practical challenges of building with factory-made components at the time.

 

The house uses many of the construction principles developed by Jean Prouvé. Prefabricated sandwich panels contain an insulating core beneath ribbed aluminum surfaces, while broad expanses of glazing sit behind slender metal columns along the garden-facing facade. Laminated wood elements support the roof, which is finished in folded aluminum.

Maison Prouvé 58 has been reconstructed near Saint-Paul-de-Vence in southern France

 

 

a compact interior furnished with modernist design

 

Inside Maison Prouvé 58, a central corridor separates the private rooms from the shared living spaces. Two bedrooms and a playroom occupy the western side, arranged around a bathroom, while the kitchen and living areas gather around a fireplace to the east. The house spans approximately 115 square meters, with generous windows connecting its compact interior to the surrounding garden.

 

Wood introduces warmth against the metal construction, and the open connections between rooms give the modest plan a sense of space. The broad glazing also brings daylight deep into the house, where the slender structural elements leave much of the surrounding landscape visible.

 

The interiors are furnished with works by Jean Prouvé and Charlotte Perriand, alongside pieces by Pierre Jeanneret and Le Corbusier. A Prouvé desk and daybed share the rooms with Perriand’s Nuage shelving, extending the architectural story into the furnishings themselves. These objects belong to Bonnet’s wider collection of 20th-century design, though here they return to their intended purpose as furniture within a home.

the prefabricated residence was originally commissioned in 1958 for a site in Alsace

 

 

a second life for modernist architecture in southern france

 

Maison Prouvé 58 now forms part of a nearly 4,000-square-meter estate alongside Le Clos du Père François, a traditional Provençal farmhouse dating to around 1850. The older residence has also been restored, with interiors furnished primarily by Jean-Michel Wilmotte and contemporary artworks throughout. Its stone construction provides a striking contrast to the thin industrial envelope of its modernist neighbor.

 

Outside, landscaped gardens connect the two buildings, with a swimming pool set against the surrounding greenery. A large wall painting by Swiss artist Claudia Comte introduces another contemporary element to the property, which forms part of Bonnet’s wider collection of art-filled residences.

 

Fondation CAB inaugurated the two properties in October 2026, opening them for stays through Les Maisons CAB. Maison Prouvé 58 accommodates four guests across two bedrooms and is rented together with the adjoining farmhouse. More than six decades after its first assembly in Alsace, the house has been taken apart and built again, giving a new purpose to a structure originally designed around the possibilities of prefabrication.

SABINE MARCELIS EXPLORES LIGHT AND COLOR IN ZURICH

 

Mathmos lava lamps stretch into an apparently endless landscape, familiar IKEA lights form a walk-through tunnel, and moving reflections bring the rhythms of sunlight and water indoors. At Museum für Gestaltung Zürich, Sabine Marcelis brings these encounters together in Light and Color, her first museum exhibition. Running from October 30, 2026, to April 4, 2027, at the museum’s Toni-Areal venue, the show combines key works from recent years with installations developed specifically for its galleries. Across furniture, lighting, mirrors, and spatial interventions, the Dutch designer explores how glass, resin, stone, and other materials capture and transform light, giving even substantial objects an unexpectedly elusive presence.

 

The exhibition moves between individual objects and environments that surround the visitor, with material samples and studies offering a closer look at the experiments behind the finished works. ‘What I wanted to create was not a retrospective in the traditional sense but a series of spatial experiences that show how light and colour behave across different scales and materials,’ Marcelis explains. A restrained exhibition design gives those effects room to unfold, allowing shifts in transparency, reflection, and color to guide the experience. ‘Some works are presented individually so you can really focus on their material and optical properties. Others are arranged as full room-sized installations where light and colour permeate the entire space.’

Exhibition Sabine Marcelis—Light and Color at Museum für Gestaltung Zürich, Oktober 30, 2026 – April 4, 2027, Photo: Umberto Romito & Ivan Šuta, Museum für Gestaltung Zürich/ZHdK

 

 

MATHMOS LAVA COLUMNS MULTIPLY IN A MIRRORED LANDSCAPE

 

Developed through Marcelis’s ongoing collaboration with British manufacturer Mathmos, Mathmos Lava Columns gives the lava lamp considerably more room to perform. Eight columns, each standing 2.56 meters high, occupy a mirrored environment conceived for the exhibition. Their reflections extend the installation into an apparently infinite field, while pastel Bubblegum pink lava rises, merges, and falls inside frosted glass bottles. White aluminum bases and caps keep the surrounding structure visually quiet, directing attention toward the softly diffused movement. ‘I always wanted to design a giant lava lamp, that was the thought I had in my very first meeting with Mathmos,’ Marcelis says.

 

The collaboration builds on a shared interest in light, color, and translucency, drawing on Mathmos’s manufacturing expertise at its factory in Poole, Dorset, where Marcelis visited to approve the final prototypes. ‘The lava lamp is one of the only objects that has light, colour and translucency all in one. It does what I try to do with resin and glass, but it adds the dimension of time and movement,’ she explains. In Zürich, that movement expands from an object’s interior to the surrounding room through the mirrored walls. The installation’s Bubblegum palette also carries into a new limited-edition Astro lamp launching in November, translating the collaboration back to a domestic scale.

Mathmos and Sabine Marcelis share a fascination with light and color | image by Mathmos

 

 

IKEA’S VARMBLIXT TUNNEL MEETS SUNBEAM CAPTURED

 

Elsewhere, Marcelis gives another recognizable lighting design an architectural role. Developed with IKEA, the VARMBLIXT collection comprises around twenty objects, with the circle emerging as a recurring motif and its ring-shaped lamp becoming particularly recognizable. For the exhibition, 125 lamps combine with a gradient mirror wall to create an eleven-meter-long, walk-through light tunnel. Repetition transforms a mass-produced object into an environment with its own depth and rhythm. As with the Mathmos columns, the mirror actively reshapes the perceived space, allowing a familiar product to exceed the dimensions of its everyday setting.

 

Created with Boris Acket, Sunbeam Captured turns to the fleeting interaction between sunlight and water. Mechanically generated waves ripple across a thin layer of water while a moving light source traces the passage of the sun. Colored reflections fill the space, amplified by a mirror that shifts from blue to orange in a gradient inspired by light’s changing color temperatures. Alongside the glowing tunnel and flowing lava, the installation gives movement a central role in Marcelis’s exploration of materials. Visitors encounter objects whose appearance keeps changing, making time as much a part of the exhibition as glass, color, or light.

West Coast modernism has a distinct flavor—that blurring of lines between architecture and nature so exalted by mid-century practitioners is made manifest here. The style is most associated with California, especially the Pacific Palisades, but also much appreciated in British Columbia, where the weather, while not quite as warm year-round, is mostly mild. From north to south, the backdrop of ocean, mountains, and urban life makes for a compelling canvas for indoor-outdoor living. In Victoria, B.C., a home designed by architecture firm BoForm with interiors by Bidgood channels the best of California modernism while adapting to its rugged, nature-embedded site.

Photography by MMK Creative

Perched above the water with a view of the Salish Sea toward the U.S. mainland, the 400-square-meter home spans three levels that cascade along the hillside. While bringing the outdoors in—through expansive glazing around the home’s perimeter—was paramount, so was imbuing the spaces within with a contemporary take on mid-century modernism.

Photography by MMK Creative

The primary living spaces are arrayed across the main floor: the living and dining areas, the main kitchen and butler’s kitchen, a powder room, and the garage. Step down to the level below, and you enter the more private spaces, including the primary bedroom (and walk-in closet), a guest bedroom with an ensuite bathroom, a home office, another powder room, and more.

Photography by MMK Creative

The lowest level is dedicated to guests and wellness; here, there are two additional guest rooms, a gym, a full bathroom, and mechanical space. The gym and guest rooms open onto a terrace with an outdoor kitchen, hot tub, and lounge area.

Photography by MMK Creative

The rooms are characterized by exposed beams, brick walls, quality millwork, and furnishings. “With the architecture and space plan established, our role was to refine the interior—developing all interior elements and ensuring a cohesive, intentional flow from the exterior inward,” says Kyla Bidgood, creative director and founder of Bidgood.

Photography by MMK Creative

Terrazzo, stone, and wood complement the blond brick, which also wraps the benches along the windows and the fireplace. The washrooms and millwork became “moments of expression,” where the design team opted for bolder surfaces and more vivid palettes.

Photography by MMK Creative

The owners, originally from Quebec, sought “warmth, earthiness, and a strong sense of place,” says Bidgood. Her firm delivered on crafting an expansive yet homey setting, where the owners’ own impressive art collection completes the scene.

Why Studio Atipus designed a wine label to look like it's been shot from a speeding train

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The design for Maria Rigol Ordi's first still wine takes the name's literal meaning and runs with it.

Creatives often wax lyrical about finding inspiration in the most unlikely of places: a rusty shipping container, a 1970s cookbook, the pattern on their nan's old curtains. All of which is lovely. But sometimes the best, most obvious ideas are right in front of you. And having the confidence to simply reach out and take it is a creative skill in itself.

Here's a great example. When Barcelona studio Atipus was asked to create the label for a wine called Vinya Llarga, it didn't have to look further than the name.

Vinya Llarga translates from Catalan as "long vineyard". It's the first still wine from Maria Rigol Ordi, a producer based in Sant Sadurní d'Anoia in the Penedès region of Catalonia, known for specialising in cava.

As Fabiola Ureña of Atipus explains, that made it a big deal for the brand. "The challenge for this launch product, the first in a new territory, was to design a label that could reflect a new chapter with the same care for craft, but with a fresh point of view."

The main feature of the label is something that's literally "long": a band of yellow, green and blue streaks that runs right around the bottle. It looks a bit like a photo taken from a fast-moving train: you can just about make out a landscape, but mostly you see bands of colour. On the back label, the streaks resolve into something leafy and green, as if the scene is coming back into focus.

Sense of restraint

Importantly, the other elements in the design are deliberately restrained. The words "Vinya Llarga" sit in small, condensed capitals at the top of the textured white label, with the producer's name, Vins tranquils de Maria Rigol Ordi, running along the bottom.

Details including the grape, fermentation and the size of the limited run are set in small type in between, along with a nod to the vineyard's history dating back to 1897.

As Fabiola explains: "The bold colour gives the bottle a strong presence, creating immediate visual impact without losing its sense of warmth. The wax-sealed capsule adds a tactile, handcrafted detail. Together, these elements strike a balance between tradition and a more expressive, modern attitude."

"A simple idea allowed us to build a wine with character, rhythm and a playful edge," she concludes, "giving Vinya Llarga a distinctive presence while keeping the wine at the heart of it all."

This mix began as a Halloween party mix (complete with “Monster Mash”) and then morphed into something weirder and scarier, I think: a mix of songs (mostly) about being crazy — maybe even psychotically! —  in love. (The more you listen to love songs the more you realize, “Man, this person is not well!”)

These mixes usually begin with what I call “seed songs” — a song that you kind of want to build a whole mix around. In this case it was the Sonics’ “Psycho” and Girls’ “Lust for Life,” which I couldn’t get out of my head for weeks. I also found that picture of Frankenstein’s monster with the little girl by the lake and these lyrics became more relevant: “I wish I had a father / And maybe then I would’ve turned out right / But now I’m just crazy / I’m totally mad…”

Another thing I learned from this mix: I really build theses mixes based on vibe, so if I have a band that captures the vibe I’m going for, I can use their influences to build up variety in the mix. For example, The Cramps’ “psychobilly” thing is what I wanted for the first side, so I simply searched “cramps influences” and got this Apple playlist that had more of the sort of stuff I was going for. Having the original versions of songs the Cramps covered (like Charlie Feathers’ “Can’t Hardly Stand It”) just thickens the stew, so to speak. (Same for searching for “Creedence covers” — this playlist popped up.)

I filled the tiny gaps on each side with Uncle Monty’s “I mean to have you, even if it must be burglary!” monologue from Withnail & I and a snippet of Edna St. Vincent Millay’s “Love is Not All.”

After more than a decade online, Lulu and Georgia has opened its first brick-and-mortar showroom in Los Angeles, bringing its eclectic approach to home furnishings to Melrose Avenue in West Hollywood’s Design District. Designed in partnership with LA-based designer Kevin Klein, the space trades conventional retail displays for a series of inviting, residential-inspired rooms where visitors can experience the brand’s collections firsthand.

Throughout the showroom, traditional architectural details pair with contemporary furnishings, sculptural lighting, and unexpected patterns. A blue-and-burgundy striped sofa anchors one living area, while warm wood finishes, upholstered seating, and carefully arranged objects lend the interiors a collected, lived-in quality. Elsewhere, floral wallpaper, soft pink textiles, and richly textured fabrics create a cozy bedroom setting.

Photography courtesy of Lulu and Georgia

“The combination of traditional and contemporary materiality and details was inspired by what you might find in a recently renovated flat in Milan or Paris,” Klein explains. “You would find heritage-inspired architectural materiality and details converging with a more modern architectural vocabulary and construction sensibility.”

That residential sensibility extends beyond the furniture. Rather than relying on high-intensity track lighting and repetitive displays, Klein incorporated decorative fixtures from Lulu and Georgia’s collections and varied the materials and finishes throughout. Even the traditional checkout counter was eliminated in favor of more hospitality-oriented points of interaction.

Photography courtesy of Lulu and Georgia

The interplay of old and new begins at the entrance, where a custom floral-inspired mosaic tile floor sits beneath a contemporary steel-framed skylight. For Klein, the pairing captures the design’s underlying vision, with natural light washing across the intricate flooring to create a memorable first impression.

Photography courtesy of Lulu and Georgia

The showroom also brings together the brand’s bestsellers, recent launches, and designer collaborations, along with a dedicated library of fabric and material swatches. Visitors can explore finishes up close or book complimentary consultations with the in-house design team for guidance on everything from floor plans to color palettes.

Photography courtesy of Lulu and Georgia

A courtyard mural by longtime collaborator Sarah Sherman Samuel introduces another artistic layer, while planned events, designer-led discussions, and intimate gatherings aim to establish the showroom as a destination for the local design community.

For a brand that has spent years cultivating its aesthetic online, the new space offers something a screen cannot: the chance to experience how its furniture, materials, and objects come together in a room.

The Roundup: Lessons on typography from Tom Foley, Ellen Lupton, and Jonathon Hoefler

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

I began my career in physical product design, and apart from a few introductory studio design classes I took while studying product design as an undergrad (in what was technically an engineering major), I didn’t have much grounding in typography or graphic design.

Over the years I got more interested in it, and picked up books like Ellen Lupton’s Graphic Design: The New Basics. But it wasn’t until I decided to start an underwater photography magazine with a friend that I challenged myself to approach it more professionally.

By the time we went to print with the first issue, I was confident that the results were, as Larry David might say, pretty, pretty good. How wrong I was.

The first issue of Wetpixel Quarterly. The cover design was OK (although I didn’t realize the UPC code could be on the back!) but don’t look too closely at the typography inside 🙈

After sharing the print version with a friend who had studied graphic design in college,1 he pointed out several fundamental errors I’d made in typography and layout. First of all, the text across the columns wasn’t aligned to the grid. There were several other noob mistakes, but the one that, in retrospect, was the biggest missed opportunity was my choice of typefaces.

I ended up using what were essentially some of the default options in Adobe’s InDesign (Minion Pro and Myriad Pro). Not terrible, but not especially unique or creative either. Perhaps the equivalent of the non-offensive invitation your great aunt might send out for her dog’s 15th birthday. By the final issue, the design and layout had gotten better, but I’m sure there are still plenty of mistakes that better designers than me would point out.

In later years, as I continued to do graphic design work for clients both in print and on the web, I learned about resources like Jonathan Hoefler’s site (now Typography.com), which offered lovely type pairings.

Fast forward another few years, and we’ve been lucky enough to have some of these typography legends on Design Better: Ellen Lupton, Jonathan Hoefler, and most recently, Tom Foley.

In this issue of The Roundup, we’ll explore what we learned from these experts, and what you might apply to your own design practice, whether you’re a beginner, intermediate, or expert.

1. Pair by structure first, personality second

Most of us pair type by what we’ve seen before—a vibe we liked: I dig how this designer paired this serif with this sans. Tom’s advice is to ignore the vibe for a minute and look at the skeleton.

“There’s some really good principles that can make it a lot easier,” he told us. “And they’re actually looking at less expressive stuff and more structural things.”

One test is ancestry—tracing each typeface back to the tools that shaped it:

“Say you’ve got a serif headline typeface and it’s a modern or a Bodoni style, so has…high contrast, and the shoulders blend into the stems and it has fine serifs. That typeface model, that modern model, eventually evolved into grotesk typefaces… So those typefaces always pair better with grotesques because they have the same structure.”

Mid-century designers like Josef Müller-Brockmann used grotesk typefaces extensively in their work.

In other words, ask “what structural aspects of this typeface have come from which calligraphic tradition? Broad nib or pointed nib.” For a deep dive on that idea, Gerrit Noordzij’s The Stroke: Theory of Writing (1985) is the source of the “translation versus expansion” framework Tom alluded to.

UX Maps: Glossary

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Summary: Use this glossary to quickly clarify key terms and concepts related to UX maps.


In UX, a map is a visual representation of an experience, process, or system that helps teams build a shared understanding .

UX practitioners create maps throughout a project to synthesize research, align stakeholders, uncover gaps in a service, and plan future work. Because many maps look alike, and some go by several names, it's easy to confuse them or to use the same term to mean different things.

There are four key concepts you need to know to get started with Penpot layouts:

1: Static layouts

By default in Penpot, layouts are static. You can drag elements around the canvas or use the X and Y coordinates to position them. If you select multiple elements at once, you can align and distribute them using the buttons at the top of the Design panel.

Hold down Alt or press the .  keyboard shortcut to view a design element’s measurements.

2: Boards

As Penpot’s canvas is infinite, a board (sometimes called “frames” in other design platforms) becomes your container. It can represent a page, a view, a screen, or anything else you need it to be! Boards can have a fixed height or width, or fit the height and width of their contents for responsive layouts.

Find the board tool in the Toolbar, or use the B keyboard shortcut.

When you create a board, a collection of preset sizes are shown in the Design panel.

3: Flex layouts

Flex layouts are ideal for unpredictable quantities or sizes of content. You can choose their flex direction (horizontally like a row or vertically like a column), choose how their contents align and are distributed, and the spacing around and in between. You can even set Flex layouts to wrap to the next row or column when their contents exceed the width or height of their container.

4: Grid layouts

Grid layouts are useful when you have a fixed amount of content, and want to define your layout across both horizontal and vertical directions at once. You define the number of columns and rows in your grid and their dimensions. Like Flex layouts, you can also choose the direction, alignment, distribution and spacing. Unlike Flex layouts, you can make grid cells span multiple columns and rows, and label grid areas.

To create a Flex or Grid layout, select a board or group and use the + button alongside Layouts in the Design panel to choose between Flex and Grid. Or use Shift + A for Flex layout, or Ctrl/Cmd + Shift + A for Grid layout.

Flex and Grid layouts use CSS Flexbox and CSS Grid, and you can get the code (CSS properties and full CSS) for these layouts from the Inspect panel. Your Flex and Grid layouts will work the same in the browser as they do in Penpot.

UX professionals advocate on users’ behalf. Empathy maps, widely used across Agile and design teams, are a simple and durable tool for both of these goals.

Definition: Empathy Map

An empathy map is a collaborative visualization that captures what we know about a particular user or about a user segment. It externalizes knowledge about users to create a shared understanding of user needs and aid in decision making.

In November 1967 Italo Calvino gave a lecture called Cybernetics and Ghosts where he talked about writing machines and the future of literature:

...will we have a machine capable of replacing the poet and the author? Just as we already have machines that can read [...] will we also have machines capable of conceiving and composing poems and novels?

I am thinking of a writing machine that would bring to the page all those things that we are accustomed to consider as the most jealously guarded attributes of our psychological life, of our daily experience, our unpredictable changes of mood and inner elations, despairs and moments of illumination.

I didn’t expect Calvino (of all folks!) to be comforted by a machine that could write If on Winter’s Night A Traveler, but ok:

...what I instinctively feel is a sense of relief, of security. The same sort of relief and sense of security that I feel every time I discover that a mess of vague and indeterminate lines turns out to be a precise geometric form; or every time I succeed in discerning a series of facts, and choices to be made out of a finite number of possibilities, in the otherwise shapeless avalanche of events. Faced with the vertigo of what is countless, unclassifiable, in a state of flux, I feel reassured by what is finite, “discrete,” and reduced to a system.

So if a computer can take writing, language, and the messiness of putting words together and do that work for us, Calvino believes it might remove all the dark magic out of literature. Or if a machine can process writing into a series of operations like a program then making a novel or a poem won’t be as frightening for us? That’s a weird take that I don’t fully get it, but it’s an interesting idea I guess?

I have to admit that whenever folks start to talk like this I feel this desperate need to defend personhood, humanity, novels, the humanities, liberal arts, books, all of it. My hackles go up reading this stuff because it’s usually coming from folks who don’t know anything about writing or literature and it feels like they’re stomping all over a vulnerable and delicate thing that they don’t understand.

But when Calvino says it we ought to pay attention.

Although, when he finds solace in literature being reduced into a series of rules and regulations, I feel great relief knowing that this is impossible. I love the mystery of novel-making. I want it to be this impossible thing. I hate all the rules and regulations of writing and want to break them in the same way that all great novels do. There are no rules! And that, to me, is liberating, exciting even.

But just as I was about to dismiss Italo as a traitor to the cause—a backstabbing, synth-sympathizer—he does the rather lovely thing that Italo always does. He hops around, tries this idea on for a moment, before jumping out of a closet with an altogether opposite-shaped idea that makes you smile:

The struggle of literature is in fact a struggle to escape from the confines of language; it stretches out from the utmost limits of what can be said; what stirs literature is the call and attraction of what is not in the dictionary.

After this 180, Calvino leaves the lecture in an oddly hopeful place by the end. He wants the machine to remove the mystery out of literature, to tell us all the rules and copy all the styles, only so that we can see those rules clearly. Then, Calvino argues, we can study them, break them.

And make something entirely new.

When the Work Looks Back At You

📅 Jun 06, 2018  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A few weeks ago, the folks at Milanote asked to interview me and brought along an unexpected brief: “let’s sit down and talk through the process of one of your projects.” As someone who is consistently banging the drum about the importance of a diligent process, it was a good chance to step back from the day-to-day workings of the studio and get a better perspective on what’s changed in my creative process in the last few years.

The most important change, I think, has been a commitment to staying open to new ideas for a longer period of time. Two-thirds through is where the good surprises tend to happen. I decided to talk with Milanote about the last project that pleasantly surprised me like this: a poster I designed for my lecture in Memphis this past March with Creative Works.

Milanote has posted a really nice write-up to their blog, and I’ve reproduced my answers in full below. Thanks to everyone at Milanote for asking good questions and giving me the opportunity to think it all through. Thanks also to Josh Horton over at Creative Works for the invitation to speak and providing carte blanche on the poster’s design.

The poster for Creative Works, available to purchase here.

Is starting a new project like this hard? Do you feel any fear or anxiety? How do you deal with it?

Designing posters is usually a fluid and enjoyable experience, but this one was tricky because it was about me. Ugh, you know? It’s hard to get enough distance from yourself and your ideas to accurately capture them. And just to make things more challenging, the talk was about what I find fascinating and important in design—the interdependence of images and words—requiring a double distillation of my creative personality. A challenging brief to capture in an image!

I was tying myself into knots over it, but eventually a friend bopped me over the head and said I was over-thinking. They pointed out that if I liked the design, it was good enough, because the poster was a proxy for me and my ideas. What a relief! It felt like a magic trick: the heaviest design challenge became light and enjoyable by having modest expectations.

Where were you when you created this piece of work? How did that environment influence it?

The poster was an after-hours project made by lamplight. Home is probably the best place to do self-referential work like this because you’re in your cave and relaxed. You can glance at the things around your house and wonder what they all have in common. The wall above my desk has all kinds of recent discoveries taped up: quotes, postcards, visual references, and print-outs of images I find online. The wall is my real-life Pinterest. Everyone should have a wall.

It’s satisfying to rearrange things with my hands to understand them differently. I printed key slides from my presentation and taped them up alongside all the visual inspiration, and eventually stumbled into the poster’s concept by adapting an image I made weeks earlier for the middle of the deck. It was right there in front of me.

What did you not understand at the start of the project that you understood later?

Making this poster reinforced that design needs to be attractive. That’s a very simple-minded realization, but I mean “attractive” in both senses of the word. Attractive as in beautiful, yes, but also attractive as in magnetic.

Part of my job is to create energy that pulls in people. It’s always felt narrow to say that design is problem-solving; there’s more to it than that. Design can inform, yet that very often needs to begin by seducing. That’s the aspect of design I find most fascinating.

If someone tried to tackle this project, what would they get wrong at first?

I think there are two modes of designing. The first requires specificity and clarity. That route would probably say that the poster should be about me, since, you know, it’s a lecture I’m giving. But this route was a dead end for this project and things didn’t really click until I got out of the way. So I took the second road, the other mode of design that runs on suggestion, excitement, and provocation. In these cases, the design’s value comes from its ability to be attractive and create anticipation for something that isn’t completely defined—it’s all built on the promise of possibilities.

Talk about a time when you were stuck or had a “creative block” on this project. What did you do about it?

I almost always start stuck and the first part of the process is letting go of my preconceived notions that are easy to dress up as experience or expertise. You have to work back to the beginner’s mind. Other than the recommendation from my friend, the second change that really helped was to focus on words instead of images.

There isn’t much text on the poster, but the talk does have a title. “The Eye Knows” is an unfortunate pun that I find funny. It gets to the tension between images and words: they both come in through the eye, but we absorb it all across two channels. The image for the poster clicked into place once I named the talk, because then I could have images and words sitting in a conceptual relationship with each other.

What surprised you about how this project evolved?

This is dorky, but I forgot how nice it is to work on something and have it look back at you! The last couple years have been a lot of branding and interface work for me, which means I’m mostly staring at colored rectangles all day. I love it, but those shapes don’t look back. There’s a face on this poster, and as I continued to refine the design, the energy it was shooting out became more real, more warm.

How did you know when you were finished?

On assignments like this, I’m a big proponent of “once through, cleanly.” You think about your idea, sketch a little, then put some glue in your chair and bang it out in one sitting. All of my best work happens this way: posters, collages, essays, outlines for talks, and so on. The work seems to be more cohesive and its energy more concentrated and palpable. If you sit down and what’ve made is bunk, you walk away, come back later, and start over. Nothing is kept but the memory of what went wrong. It’s a silly method, but it works for me. Once through, cleanly, because there are no half karate chops.

It’s probably irresponsible to work this way when other people are involved, and it is definitely no way to write a novel, but for my smaller self-initiated projects, this method has produced the greatest rate of success. It makes it so much easier to know what you’ve got while you’re working on it: the closer you are to finishing, the more difficult your enthusiasm makes it to sit down, focus, and finish.

Did this project change you at all?

Sure! Choosing how to represent yourself and your ideas is intensely personal. I’m still surprised I’m so happy with the outcome. Maybe I can value this design a bit more honestly because it feels like it came from somewhere else. It does a good job of capturing my attitude towards design: affable, intelligent, and clear, expressing some welcome charm.
