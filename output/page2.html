DESIGNBOOM X INDX|GLOBAL: ROOM FOR UTOPIAS 

 

Produced in collaboration with INDX|GLOBAL during designboom.paris, designboom presents Room for Utopias, a podcast series that gathers Indian architects and designers who are exploring architecture as a practical method for imagining and testing alternative ways of living. Across eleven conversations filmed in Paris, the series steps away from grand, speculative urban visions to examine grounded propositions embedded in contemporary practice: shifting relationships with natural ecosystems, adaptable living structures, regional material cultures, and altered dynamics between architects, clients, and users.

 

The first article – A World that Starts with Place – brings together two podcast conversations moderated by Iñaki Laguardia, designboom’s Managing Editor. Both episodes examine an architecture that begins by listening rather than imposing: one expanding how buildings coexist with natural elements, and the other uncovering spatial, cultural, and technological intelligence already present on site.

 

 

ARCHITECTURE COEXISTING WITH NATURE AND SENSORY LIVING

 

The first episode of the Room for Utopias podcast convenes four practitioners – Anushri Patel of HPAD, Shilpi Sonar of Creations & Urban Saints, Shiny Mariaselvam of Studio Sculpt, and Ridhima Sogani of Ankit Gupta & Ridhima Sogani Architects – to re-evaluate architecture’s relationship with its environment. Rather than treating green elements as decorative infill or post-construction additions, the panel positions natural systems as active participants in spatial design.

 

 

Anushri Patel of HPAD addresses how architecture interacts with raw site conditions in regions with high ambient temperatures. Rather than treating green elements as decorative additions, Patel describes using native vegetation as a functional building instrument for indoor microclimate regulation and thermal control. Patel points to an evolving client mindset, noting an increased willingness to preserve existing site topographies – such as building around natural rock formations – rather than clearing ground for maximum built area.

 

Shilpi Sonar of Creations & Urban Saints positions nature as a baseline that exists prior to construction. Sonar argues against viewing nature as an element added after building completion, asserting that true design begins by understanding site conditions that are already present. To balance built density, Sonar advocates for designing unprogrammed, reflective spaces that allow occupants room to pause. ‘Nature cannot be added second… it’s always prevalent. We have to understand how we take architecture to it,‘ Sonar explains.

 

Shiny Mariaselvam of Studio Sculpt focuses on how spatial layouts condition daily human habits and routines. Mariaselvam emphasizes the responsibility of architectural projects to engage surrounding production systems and support generational artisans through regional material sourcing rather than importing elements across long supply chains. ‘Give space for the local industries and local people to be able to produce what they’re good at for generations,‘ Mariaselvam states.

 

Ridhima Sogani of Ankit Gupta & Ridhima Sogani Architects addresses the sensory dimension of architecture, contrasting physiological experience with visual image-making. Drawing on references like Peter Zumthor, Sogani calls on architects to design for tactile climate harmony and daily rhythms rather than purely photographic appeal. ‘When I look at a site, I listen to it. I do not ask what I want to build; I ask what the place wants to become,‘ Sogani shares.

READING SITE CONTEXT, HUMAN USERS AND TECHNOLOGICAL INTELLIGENCE

 

The second conversation expands site reading beyond physical topography. Moderator Iñaki Laguardia sits down with Dinesh Panwar of Urbanscape Architects, Hiren Patel of HPAD, and Monika Choudhary of ZHCollective | Habitat Architects to discuss how existing site conditions, end-user requirements, and emerging digital tools shape architectural decision-making.

 

 

Hiren Patel of HPAD frames built intervention as a responsible footprint on the planet’s surface. ‘Will we be able to do something beautiful on the skin of mother planet that it is not a scar on her skin, but a beautiful tattoo?‘ Patel asks. Patel integrates digital testing with physical site visits, using artificial intelligence models to cross-reference design plans against Christopher Alexander’s spatial patterns. Patel’s studio is currently developing ‘HirenBhai’, a personalized AI model trained on his studio’s archive and design methodology.

 

Monika Choudhary of ZHCollective | Habitat Architects approaches site reading through regional history and collective identity, citing the principle of Ubuntu (‘I am because we are’). In an ongoing Haveli project in Punjab, Choudhary’s practice revives regional construction techniques and traditional Nanakshahi bricks to maintain historical memory in contemporary forms. ‘If we have to build, I would love to go backward and forward—learn from the tradition, but reinterpret it in a way that the nostalgia remains, but the intelligence is carried forward,’ Choudhary says.

 

Dinesh Panwar of Urbanscape Architects discusses site integration when surrounding conditions demand spatial restraint. Confronted with designing a project adjacent to a 21-meter (70-foot) structure, Panwar’s studio submerged a 2,787-square-meter (30,000-square-foot) building below ground, using the surrounding earth and planted landscape as the primary context rather than competing visually. Panwar adds that for specialized programs like schools or industrial facilities, the human end-user replaces physical terrain as the primary context shaping spatial decisions.

1/3 Monika Choudhary (ZHCollective | Habitat Architects) Hiren Patel (HPAD) Dinesh Panwar (Urbanscape Architects)

 

SMALL ARCHITECTURAL PROPOSITIONS FOR ALTERNATIVE FUTURES

 

Through microclimate planting, local artisan networks, subterranean layouts, heritage masonry, and AI pattern testing, both conversations outline an architecture rooted in careful site observation. These projects suggest that alternative futures emerge not from top-down urban schemes, but from precise, quiet adjustments in how architects listen to physical terrain, regional culture, and human behavior before construction begins.

HERZOG & DE MEURON REVEALS EAMES INSTITUTE MUSEUM PLANS

 

A warehouse with a roof this expressive already has something to exhibit. In Novato, California, Herzog & de Meuron is reworking the former Birkenstock campus into the future museum of the Eames Institute of Infinite Curiosity, preserving its sculptural concrete silhouette while changing what happens beneath it. Newly released renderings show the proposal taking shape with San Francisco practice EHDD as executive architect and Field Operations designing the landscape. Across an approximately 88-acre site, the project brings exhibition spaces, workshops, a theater, and gardens into a cultural destination whose ambitions extend beyond the work of Charles and Ray Eames.

 

The existing campus was designed for publisher McGraw-Hill by Jan Lubicz-Nycz for the office of John Savage Bolles, before becoming associated with Birkenstock. Its most recognizable element is a roof assembled from repeated hyperbolic paraboloid shells, developed with structural engineer Raj Desai. These curved surfaces use geometry to span large distances with remarkably little material, producing a sequence of pointed edges that gives the industrial building its unexpected drama. Retaining this structure makes the museum itself an example of design ingenuity: visitors will encounter an experiment in construction before reaching the first exhibition.

the museum is targeting an opening in 2030 | renders by Herzog & De Meuron

 

 

A REFLECTIVE BRIDGE REFRAMES THE CONCRETE WAREHOUSE

 

Herzog & de Meuron organizes the interior around a generous central hall, keeping the roof’s geometry visible overhead. An entrance court cut into an existing earth berm establishes the approach, while a steel-clad bridge introduces an elevated route through the building. Its reflective finish will catch fragments of the surrounding space, setting a visually shifting surface against the weight of the concrete canopy. Below, circulation routes connect exhibitions with places to gather and work. Building services and lighting will be integrated into the ceiling to reduce the visual clutter beneath its peaks.

 

The museum’s program gives visitors opportunities to see how things are made alongside the finished works. Studios and workshops for wood and ceramics sit within an arrangement of flexible exhibition spaces, allowing creative activity to become part of the visit. This approach also informs the broader collecting strategy, which encompasses industrial design, painting, sculpture, and other disciplines. The Eames legacy provides a point of departure for a museum interested in experimentation across generations, with space for the practical, occasionally messy business that precedes an object’s arrival on a pedestal.

the former Birkenstock campus will become the Eames Institute Museum

 

 

FIELD OPERATIONS BRINGS THE EAMES MUSEUM INTO THE LANDSCAPE

 

At the building’s northern and southern edges, recessed glass façades create deep covered areas between the interiors and gardens. These sheltered thresholds extend the possibilities for exhibitions and everyday use, while former loading zones become places to spend time outdoors. Field Operations connects ten gardens through paths, introducing native species including oak, manzanita, and California poppy. Planting draws on the region’s different habitats, from woodland to coastal prairie, giving the grounds a character rooted in Marin County rather than treating them as a uniform backdrop.

 

Outdoor sculpture, places to play, and areas for communal events will share this landscape with quieter spaces for sitting and walking. The proposal allows a museum visit to unfold across different settings and at different speeds, from a workshop or performance to an afternoon outside. Following the Institute’s acquisition of the property in 2025, the masterplan has been submitted to the City of Novato. Major construction is intended to begin in 2028, with 2030 set as the target opening year.

maison prouvé 58 finds a new home near saint-paul-de-vence

 

Maison Prouvé 58, a prefabricated house developed by Jean Prouvé and his son Claude in 1958, has been reconstructed near Saint-Paul-de-Vence in southern France after being saved from demolition in Alsace. The low, L-shaped residence now stands among pine and olive trees, overlooking the Mediterranean landscape. Recently reopened through Les Maisons CAB, the house is available for overnight stays (see here).

 

The building originally stood in Epfig, a village in northeastern France, where it was commissioned by local notary Édouard Degott. It remained there for more than sixty years, undergoing several alterations along the way. When the municipality acquired the property for redevelopment, plans for a new residential care facility threatened the house with demolition.

 

Belgian art collector Hubert Bonnet acquired the structure in 2021 and arranged for its dismantling the following year. Its components were transported to Nancy for restoration before being reassembled in Provence, where its aluminum facade and long stretches of glazing now face an entirely different landscape.

images © Micha Pycke

 

 

jean and claude prouvé’s experiments in prefabricated housing

 

The original commission began with Jean Prouvé, whose experiments with industrialized construction had already produced a series of lightweight, demountable buildings. Working in Paris at the time, he entrusted the project to Claude, who developed the architectural plans and oversaw construction. The house was completed in 1959, despite the 1958 date retained in its present-day name.

 

Archival research from the École Nationale Supérieure d’Architecture de Nancy reveals an eventful construction process. Delayed deliveries of facade panels and glazing slowed work on site, and Claude eventually helped assemble the window frames himself. The difficulties offer a glimpse into the practical challenges of building with factory-made components at the time.

 

The house uses many of the construction principles developed by Jean Prouvé. Prefabricated sandwich panels contain an insulating core beneath ribbed aluminum surfaces, while broad expanses of glazing sit behind slender metal columns along the garden-facing facade. Laminated wood elements support the roof, which is finished in folded aluminum.

Maison Prouvé 58 has been reconstructed near Saint-Paul-de-Vence in southern France

 

 

a compact interior furnished with modernist design

 

Inside Maison Prouvé 58, a central corridor separates the private rooms from the shared living spaces. Two bedrooms and a playroom occupy the western side, arranged around a bathroom, while the kitchen and living areas gather around a fireplace to the east. The house spans approximately 115 square meters, with generous windows connecting its compact interior to the surrounding garden.

 

Wood introduces warmth against the metal construction, and the open connections between rooms give the modest plan a sense of space. The broad glazing also brings daylight deep into the house, where the slender structural elements leave much of the surrounding landscape visible.

 

The interiors are furnished with works by Jean Prouvé and Charlotte Perriand, alongside pieces by Pierre Jeanneret and Le Corbusier. A Prouvé desk and daybed share the rooms with Perriand’s Nuage shelving, extending the architectural story into the furnishings themselves. These objects belong to Bonnet’s wider collection of 20th-century design, though here they return to their intended purpose as furniture within a home.

the prefabricated residence was originally commissioned in 1958 for a site in Alsace

 

 

a second life for modernist architecture in southern france

 

Maison Prouvé 58 now forms part of a nearly 4,000-square-meter estate alongside Le Clos du Père François, a traditional Provençal farmhouse dating to around 1850. The older residence has also been restored, with interiors furnished primarily by Jean-Michel Wilmotte and contemporary artworks throughout. Its stone construction provides a striking contrast to the thin industrial envelope of its modernist neighbor.

 

Outside, landscaped gardens connect the two buildings, with a swimming pool set against the surrounding greenery. A large wall painting by Swiss artist Claudia Comte introduces another contemporary element to the property, which forms part of Bonnet’s wider collection of art-filled residences.

 

Fondation CAB inaugurated the two properties in October 2026, opening them for stays through Les Maisons CAB. Maison Prouvé 58 accommodates four guests across two bedrooms and is rented together with the adjoining farmhouse. More than six decades after its first assembly in Alsace, the house has been taken apart and built again, giving a new purpose to a structure originally designed around the possibilities of prefabrication.

SABINE MARCELIS EXPLORES LIGHT AND COLOR IN ZURICH

 

Mathmos lava lamps stretch into an apparently endless landscape, familiar IKEA lights form a walk-through tunnel, and moving reflections bring the rhythms of sunlight and water indoors. At Museum für Gestaltung Zürich, Sabine Marcelis brings these encounters together in Light and Color, her first museum exhibition. Running from October 30, 2026, to April 4, 2027, at the museum’s Toni-Areal venue, the show combines key works from recent years with installations developed specifically for its galleries. Across furniture, lighting, mirrors, and spatial interventions, the Dutch designer explores how glass, resin, stone, and other materials capture and transform light, giving even substantial objects an unexpectedly elusive presence.

 

The exhibition moves between individual objects and environments that surround the visitor, with material samples and studies offering a closer look at the experiments behind the finished works. ‘What I wanted to create was not a retrospective in the traditional sense but a series of spatial experiences that show how light and colour behave across different scales and materials,’ Marcelis explains. A restrained exhibition design gives those effects room to unfold, allowing shifts in transparency, reflection, and color to guide the experience. ‘Some works are presented individually so you can really focus on their material and optical properties. Others are arranged as full room-sized installations where light and colour permeate the entire space.’

Exhibition Sabine Marcelis—Light and Color at Museum für Gestaltung Zürich, Oktober 30, 2026 – April 4, 2027, Photo: Umberto Romito & Ivan Šuta, Museum für Gestaltung Zürich/ZHdK

 

 

MATHMOS LAVA COLUMNS MULTIPLY IN A MIRRORED LANDSCAPE

 

Developed through Marcelis’s ongoing collaboration with British manufacturer Mathmos, Mathmos Lava Columns gives the lava lamp considerably more room to perform. Eight columns, each standing 2.56 meters high, occupy a mirrored environment conceived for the exhibition. Their reflections extend the installation into an apparently infinite field, while pastel Bubblegum pink lava rises, merges, and falls inside frosted glass bottles. White aluminum bases and caps keep the surrounding structure visually quiet, directing attention toward the softly diffused movement. ‘I always wanted to design a giant lava lamp, that was the thought I had in my very first meeting with Mathmos,’ Marcelis says.

 

The collaboration builds on a shared interest in light, color, and translucency, drawing on Mathmos’s manufacturing expertise at its factory in Poole, Dorset, where Marcelis visited to approve the final prototypes. ‘The lava lamp is one of the only objects that has light, colour and translucency all in one. It does what I try to do with resin and glass, but it adds the dimension of time and movement,’ she explains. In Zürich, that movement expands from an object’s interior to the surrounding room through the mirrored walls. The installation’s Bubblegum palette also carries into a new limited-edition Astro lamp launching in November, translating the collaboration back to a domestic scale.

Mathmos and Sabine Marcelis share a fascination with light and color | image by Mathmos

 

 

IKEA’S VARMBLIXT TUNNEL MEETS SUNBEAM CAPTURED

 

Elsewhere, Marcelis gives another recognizable lighting design an architectural role. Developed with IKEA, the VARMBLIXT collection comprises around twenty objects, with the circle emerging as a recurring motif and its ring-shaped lamp becoming particularly recognizable. For the exhibition, 125 lamps combine with a gradient mirror wall to create an eleven-meter-long, walk-through light tunnel. Repetition transforms a mass-produced object into an environment with its own depth and rhythm. As with the Mathmos columns, the mirror actively reshapes the perceived space, allowing a familiar product to exceed the dimensions of its everyday setting.

 

Created with Boris Acket, Sunbeam Captured turns to the fleeting interaction between sunlight and water. Mechanically generated waves ripple across a thin layer of water while a moving light source traces the passage of the sun. Colored reflections fill the space, amplified by a mirror that shifts from blue to orange in a gradient inspired by light’s changing color temperatures. Alongside the glowing tunnel and flowing lava, the installation gives movement a central role in Marcelis’s exploration of materials. Visitors encounter objects whose appearance keeps changing, making time as much a part of the exhibition as glass, color, or light.

In its 26th edition, Dutch Design Week brought together more than 2,500 designers, from established names to emerging talent, alongside creative collaborations with major brands. This year’s theme, Rebel Authentic Worth (or R.A.W.), highlighted the social, cultural, and economic value of “design that creates space for experimentation, material research, and radical imagination.”

The theme placed particular emphasis on the unpolished and experimental at a time when technology and AI increasingly prioritize optimization and visual perfection. It also reflected a growing sense of uncertainty around technological advancement, exploring how design might challenge these developments, offer alternative perspectives, or help us navigate an increasingly complex world.

Here, we highlight 11 projects that stood out at Dutch Design Week 2026.

1. Design Academy Eindhoven

Design Academy Eindhoven’s annual Graduation Show offered a wide-ranging look at the next generation of design talent, with projects spanning objects, installations, and experimental research. Themes ran the gamut, including how change can bring about both gains and unintended consequences. Tradition can sustain a sense of belonging while carrying colonial or nationalist histories, for example, while emerging professions create new possibilities without necessarily guaranteeing a livelihood.

‘Safe House’ graduation project by Dirk Duif. Photography by Ronald Smits.

“This year’s graduation projects can be read as maps of how graduates have navigated contradictions and established their bearings: what to attend to, what to carry forward, what to stand for and which relationships might sustain their work,” says Nadine Botha, head of the Social Design Master’s program at DAE.

‘Excess Cornice’ graduate project by Yann Thiel. Photography by Femke Reijerman.

The exhibition’s examination of institutional systems was particularly timely. Graduating DAE students recently protested conditions at the school, citing inadequate studio space and limited facilities for making their work.

2. Fairspace × Studio Shruthi Venkat × Sagitta Creatives: Blind Spots

Large-scale installations in public spaces allow visitors to encounter and interact with design as they move through Eindhoven during Dutch Design Week. One of them, called Blind Spots, asks, “Who is safety built for?”

An AI illustration of ‘Creatives Blind Spots’ by Fairspace × Shruthi Venkat × Sagitta. Rendering courtesy of Fairspace.

Created by Fairspace, Shruthi Venkat, and Sagitta Creatives, the installation used familiar symbols of security—including surveillance cameras, screens, and construction netting—to explore the relationship between protection, surveillance, and control. Participants could wander through overlapping layers of mesh that obscured sightlines, while recorded voices offered different perspectives on what it means to feel safe.

Rendering and photography courtesy of Fairspace

Visitors were also invited to write down their experiences and tie them to the nets for others to read. As the contributions accumulated, the installation gradually became a collective record of how differently people experience safety in public spaces.

3. Ori Orisun Merhav: Woven Landscapes

In Woven Landscapes, artist and material researcher Ori Orisun Merhav presented what she called a dialogue between humans, land, and other-than-human life, with an installation of sculptural forms combining woven palm fibers and insect-produced resin. The organic structures explored how materials shaped by living organisms might influence the way objects are designed and made.

Photography by Studio Mimesis

Merhav is the founder of Made by Insects, an ongoing research practice investigating lac, a natural resin secreted by certain insects, and its potential as a material for design.

Photography courtesy of Ori Orisun Merhav

“The main practice of the studio is research,” Merhav says. “The sculptures are the leftovers, segments of the time and journey of our research.” Through weaving, binding, and coating, the resulting vessels connect traditional craft techniques with biological processes, suggesting a more collaborative relationship between human-made objects and the natural world.

4. Yinka Ilori x Brabantia: There Is Magic in Joy

Yinka Ilori is a British-Nigerian multidisciplinary artist and designer whose practice spans architecture, sculpture, furniture, and interior and graphic design. Known for his exuberant use of color and pattern, he brought that same approach to everyday household objects in a collaboration with Dutch housewares brand Brabantia.

Photography courtesy of Brabantia

For the limited-edition There Is Magic in Joy collection, Ilori sought to make daily chores more pleasurable by transforming practical items, including ironing board covers and waste bins, into playful design objects. The palette is sherbet-like orange, lemon yellow, and raspberry, with aqua accents and bold graphic patterns that give otherwise mundane products an unexpected personality.

Yinka Ilori photographed with the ‘There Is Magic in Joy’ collection. Photography courtesy of Brabantia.

“At the heart of my work is a belief that art and design should be accessible to all, with projects that bring communities together and create a positive social impact,” Ilori says.

5. TAM Industries: House of Lights

Photography courtesy of TAM Industries

Prefabricated housing is often associated with standardized designs, but Eindhoven-based TAM Industries is exploring how 3D printing could offer greater architectural freedom. Its House of Lights installation demonstrated how automated construction can accommodate more expressive forms without abandoning the efficiencies of industrial production.

Printed from recycled plastic using machines developed by TAM, the structure incorporated color directly into the material, eliminating the need for paint. Integrated lighting illuminated the printed facade after dark, transforming the house’s appearance.

The project also explored the potential for reusing its materials at the end of the structure’s life. Rather than treating automation as a constraint on creativity, TAM sees it as a way to make more varied architectural designs possible.

Photography courtesy of Rive Roshan

6. Rive Roshan: Beacons of Hope

The idea that we live in uncertain times—and how design can provide spaces for reflection and reassurance—was pervasive at the festival. Beacons of Hope by the Amsterdam-based design duo Rive Roshan (Ruben de la Rive Box and Golnar Roshan) responded to this climate with an immersive installation combining textured glass, reflective surfaces, and kinetic light.

The duo treats light as a primary material, using glass and metal surfaces to shape its movement and create shifting patterns of reflection. The installation was designed to offer visitors a moment of pause, connection, and optimism.

“We believe design has a responsibility to foster connection and offer sanctuary,” the designers say. “By choreographing kinetic light, we tap into something universal and deeply emotional, offering a counter-narrative to the heaviness of the daily news cycle and the weight on our emotional well-being.”

Photography by Maarten Van der Wolf

7. Gijs Schalkx: Onderweg naar een toekomst [On the Way to a Future]

“Ever since I can remember, I have been curious about how things work,” artist Gijs Schalkx says. That curiosity has led him to build an unconventional collection of vehicles, including a motorcycle powered by swamp gas, a car that runs on waste plastic, and an electric flying bicycle.

Presented as Onderweg naar een toekomst (On the Way to a Future), the functioning vehicles question our assumptions about efficiency, progress, and sustainability. “Technology is often presented as infallible, fast and efficient. I feel that most people take it in as passive consumers, without any awareness of the processes and infrastructures behind it,” he says.

How do I exorcise a nightmare client and ensure they don't rise from the dead?

With Halloween almost upon us, we've picked a Dear Boom missive that reads like the opening of a horror film.

A creative writes: "I've been working with a client for nearly two years. Every job starts as 'something really simple'. The brief changes halfway through. Finished projects come back from the dead. And somehow, I always end up agreeing to one more thing. I've been calling it a learning experience. But I think I've had enough now."

"The good news is, I'm ready to leave. But then I try to write the email. I've softened the latest draft so much it now sounds like I'm not trying to move away at all. I want to leave professionally, wish them well and experience the enormous pleasure of never receiving another 'quick question' from them on a Sunday evening, ever again."

So how do you make a clean exit when you're very good at saying yes and bad at saying goodbye? We put the question to our community on Instagram and The Studio. A couple of you suggested calling in an exorcist (Keiron Lewis helpfully notes you'll need it authorised by a bishop), but most had rather more practical advice...

1. Goodbye or renegotiation?

A few of you asked whether a break-up is actually necessary. Designer Nicholas Robb suggested explaining the problem in plain language and proposing a fix, such as fixed briefs or higher fees, arguing that you have nothing to lose. Illustrator and animator Csaba Klement suggested updating your contract so that changes cost money: "If they won't sign, your exit is clearly on them."

These sound like good options in some situations. But we'll believe our correspondent when they say they've firmly decided to leave. After all, if you'd be secretly disappointed when they said yes, any negotiation won't be a win, but a potential own goal.

2. Check your contract

As illustrator Charlotte Goodman-Brooks points out, when you break up with a client, check your contract first. If there's a notice period, honour it.

If there's no contract, though, creative director and consultant Paul Leon suggests you owe nothing beyond finishing what you agreed to. "I'd do my last project and walk away," he says. "You don't have to explain anything; you don't have to metaphorically pour petrol on the bridge and set it on fire, unless you really want to make a point.

3. Explain, but don't litigate

The biggest split in our discussions was over whether to give reasons at all. Designer Matthew Gallagher argues you don't owe anyone an explanation. "Merely let them know that your schedule is booked and you won't be able to take any more work from them," he recommends. "It gives them a heads-up, and you can move on by being gracious and without having to burn any bridges."

Photo retoucher and creative artworker Sandrine Bascouert, however, takes the view that the truth can be one last service to a client who's been surrounded by yes-people and has no idea they're a nightmare. "You don't have to be harsh or rude," she says. "Just tell it like it is, keep it short and make it clear there's no coming back."

Illustrator Lucila Solier Feiguin agrees. "You should probably give them the reasons, at least broadly speaking ('due to X/Y/Z the situation has escalated beyond the agreed budget and contract multiple times, which has made me take a step backwards to reassess')," she says. "Try focusing on the facts and what kind of consequences those had."

4. Writing the exit email

Now for what some might consider the biggest challenge: emailing the client. Designer Jessica Rebelo knows exactly where our correspondent is coming from. "I'm a people pleaser and have been there, accepting a lot of work, sometimes for very little money," she admits. "It's tricky!"

Lucila's tip is to put yourself in the shoes of "the boss" when you write the email, and allow yourself to state your boundaries. Then, crucially, don't undo them in the sign-off. "Don't add anything like 'but we could work together again', or you'll be basically telling them that your boundaries mean nothing," she warns.

A good exit email needs to tick three boxes. The client needs to clearly understand you won't be taking on further work. You need to define an end point: when you've caught up with the work you owe them, and you're done. And finally, a sincere good wish—but one that acts as a full stop, rather than a signal for further chat.

Madalena Studio did exactly this last year. "We said that our business focus was moving in a different direction and, whilst we had personally enjoyed and greatly appreciated working with them and their team, that they might be better finding a partner who was more aligned with their needs," the studio explains. "We politely gave them a four-week notice period, wished them well and sent their files over at the end."

Hand-painting illustrator Lee Gruber took a similar approach when ending a retainer. "I gave a month's notice, kept scope firm, and offered a handover with a clear end date," he says. "I even offered alternatives to fill my role."

5. The happy ending

Here's the part our correspondent will enjoy most. After all that agonising, how did Madalena Studio's client of four years respond? Quite simply: "I am aligned on this."

It often goes like this. You spend all that time pent up, dreading your reaction, and then nothing happens. It's anti-climactic for sure. But on the plus side, it leaves you to get on with your life, and that has to be a good thing.

The final part of this horror story? Make sure the sequel never gets made. As Paul stresses, solid contracts, terms, and work schedules protect you next time. Designer and founder Brian Winkeler goes further, telling every new client his agency has two set-in-stone rules. "We don't work with jerks," he says. "Anyone who can say 'No' must be part of the process." Do that, and your next horror story might never make it past the trailer.

Amsterdam's Straat offers a masterclass in the tricky balancing act of bringing graffiti out of the streets and into the museum: its new show of Henry Chalfant's sublime documentation of New York's nascent 1980s scene does it beautifully.

The art world, and society far more broadly, has had a tempestuous relationship with "street art" over the decades. It's been met with pretty much every response imaginable: in the early days, publicly denigrated by hand-wringing Mary Whitehouse types as a scourge on society, a symbol of ASBO youth writ large on walls, trains and the backs of bus seats.

Later on, street art – in certain specific pockets of the world at least – was firmly cemented into the commercial art world; a signifier of a certain pre-2008-crash, Shoreditchy hipsterness, when it was par for the course for street art to be shown in galleries (some with more longevity than others).

Not long after those wildly carefree, neon-and-powder-sprinkled years, though, street art seemed to lose its hip sheen. A friend of mine has long quipped that her number one red flag in a man is if she "goes back to his place and finds the Banksy coffee table book proudly on show". An arguably irrational ick, but I get it: I'd be straight out the door if a potential amour expressed a love of the Red Hot Chili Peppers, or, even worse, called them "the Chillis" (shudder). But I digress.

All of this is, of course, a highly truncated, reductive and rather subjective overview of a vastly expansive movement, one that deserves much more. And while trends are fleeting, something that remains a constant is how difficult it is to show art born in and of the streets in a gallery or museum. Safe to say, Straat in Amsterdam has absolutely nailed it.

A familiar post-industrial trajectory

The vast Straat Museum building – formerly the site where huge ships were built – sits on the NDSM (Nederlandsche Dok en Scheepsbouw Maatschappij, a former Dutch shipbuilding company) wharf in Amsterdam Noord, a short ferry ride across the water from Centraal Station.

The shipyard closed in 1984, and the site lay derelict; then a trajectory familiar to most cities began to unfold: buildings were squatted and then colonised by artists, and as such, things were beginning to be vaguely patched up into live-work-ish spaces throughout the 90s. Today, those huge, arguably brutishly industrially ugly warehouse buildings have evolved into legit art studios, event spaces and a café.

The Kunststad ("art city"), for instance, houses hundreds of artists, architects and designers; shipping containers have become student homes; and a former shipyard crane is now a hotel.

While the dwellings themselves vary hugely in both aesthetics and function, what's common to the entire area is the prevalence of street art: murals climb entire walls; tags abound; most walls have been decorated in one way or another (the Anne Frank mural on Straat's front is now a well-established landmark, visible from far across the water); kids come from far and wide to find the wall/canvases where they can freely learn their craft without being moved along by the authorities.

The area reminds me a bit of Hackney Wick about 15 years ago. The industrial past is celebrated rather than hidden: big warehouses many might deem eyesores, but embellished and enlivened by the creative types who first venture into these former urban wastelands and, inadvertently, lay the ground for their gentrification (the high-rises and corporate tenants now arriving on NDSM wharf suggest that process is well under way).

From shipbuilding yard to flea market to museum

The story of how Straat came to be is almost as colourful as its walls. It began as a flea market: the founder was asked to add some art to the venue, invited graffiti writers in, and was so struck by the public response that he decided to build a museum. He started building without the right permits. "I'd rather ask for forgiveness than full permission," is a line that Straat educator Alex Pope says sums up the approach.

Straat opened in 2020 – the worst possible timing thanks to Covid – and the founder's three daughters even remortgaged their houses to keep the venture afloat, a testament to their belief in the museum and the good it does.

It's hard to describe just how overwhelming, awe-inspiringly vast Straat Museum itself is: so much so that you barely notice a literal truck suspended from the ceiling, such is the way it's dwarfed by the scale of the walls around it.

Street art's foundations in socioeconomic hardship are the palpable throughline of Straat's current exhibition, Art Is Not a Crime, a survey of the photography of Henry Chalfant. Photography and documentation matter a hell of a lot for graffiti and street art – more than pretty much any other art form – for the simple fact of their inherent fleetingness.

The star of the show here is Chalfant's extensive documentation of graffiti on New York trains – at Straat, the images are presented life/train size, on metallic paper, with a huge backlit screen showing cars "running" into the station. "The profile they saw was that of a middle-aged white man, exactly like most of the cops," he says. "I looked like a cop, so people stayed away."

Chalfant grew up in privileged circumstances and had never had to defend himself. It took one kid, curious that this man seemed uninterested in him, to come over and ask what he was doing. Chalfant always made sure to have some of his snaps to hand – he showed them to the kid, who instantly recognised his cousin's piece in one of the images.

The exhibition is a triumph, and not an easy one in a building like Straat which spans 8,000 square metres, 2,000 of which are given over to Chalfant's show. Presenting photographs of graffiti-laden trains as big as trains themselves feels like exactly the right response.

And if ever there was a good time to be showing work like this, it's now: a timeline on display in Straat itself traces the origins of graffiti back to student protests – and look at what's happening in Paris, and around the world right now.

The beauty of street art – no matter its setting, its more recent position as an art market commodity, its coffee table books and repurposing as nothing but a 'cool' Instagram background and icks; its format or function or medium or message – is that it's born of disillusionment, of trying to say something, of giving voice in the most public of public settings to those people who don't usually get to be heard.

Creatives often wax lyrical about finding inspiration in the most unlikely of places: a rusty shipping container, a 1970s cookbook, the pattern on their nan's old curtains. All of which is lovely. But sometimes the best, most obvious ideas are right in front of you. And having the confidence to simply reach out and take it is a creative skill in itself.

Here's a great example. When Barcelona studio Atipus was asked to create the label for a wine called Vinya Llarga, it didn't have to look further than the name.

Vinya Llarga translates from Catalan as "long vineyard". It's the first still wine from Maria Rigol Ordi, a producer based in Sant Sadurní d'Anoia in the Penedès region of Catalonia, known for specialising in cava.

As Fabiola Ureña of Atipus explains, that made it a big deal for the brand. "The challenge for this launch product, the first in a new territory, was to design a label that could reflect a new chapter with the same care for craft, but with a fresh point of view."

The main feature of the label is something that's literally "long": a band of yellow, green and blue streaks that runs right around the bottle. It looks a bit like a photo taken from a fast-moving train: you can just about make out a landscape, but mostly you see bands of colour. On the back label, the streaks resolve into something leafy and green, as if the scene is coming back into focus.

Importantly, the other elements in the design are deliberately restrained. The words "Vinya Llarga" sit in small, condensed capitals at the top of the textured white label, with the producer's name, Vins tranquils de Maria Rigol Ordi, running along the bottom.

Details including the grape, fermentation and the size of the limited run are set in small type in between, along with a nod to the vineyard's history dating back to 1897.

As Fabiola explains: "The bold colour gives the bottle a strong presence, creating immediate visual impact without losing its sense of warmth. The wax-sealed capsule adds a tactile, handcrafted detail. Together, these elements strike a balance between tradition and a more expressive, modern attitude."

"A simple idea allowed us to build a wine with character, rhythm and a playful edge," she concludes, "giving Vinya Llarga a distinctive presence while keeping the wine at the heart of it all."
