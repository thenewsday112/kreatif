How Mother Design turned a séance into a new identity for Purple Garden

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The London studio began rebranding the psychic services platform with a chakra reading and a little charcoal, and the result is a rich, purple-hued brand world that feels alive.

It's almost Halloween, so forgive me if, on discovering that a branding project began with a séance, I immediately thought of that brilliant 'Day-O' dinner party scene in Beetlejuice. You know, the one with the late great Catherine O'Hara? I presume no one at Mother Design was forced into an impromptu calypso number, but it's a wonderful mental image.

What actually happened involved way less dancing and uncontrolled lip syncing. The independent design studio, part of the Mother Family, has crafted a new identity for Purple Garden, the mobile and online psychic services platform. Fittingly, the team decided to lean into a more spiritual approach to get there.

The brief was to "bring the feeling of spiritual practice into the digital world", which was no easy task. The spiritual services industry is worth around $2.3 billion, and apps and online platforms are opening up new ways for people to connect with guidance. But things can get lost in translation on screen: the atmosphere, ritual, and energy – it's hard to replicate online when you're not in the room. Mother Design's job was to fix that.

The new brand therefore reframes Purple Garden from somewhere you visit for a one-off reading into a home for ongoing spiritual practice. It's built on a rather lovely insight: some questions keep returning no matter how much life changes. The audience, which Mother calls 'Believers of the Bigger Picture', are "seasoned seekers" looking for growth and meaning. So the creative idea, 'A Living Practice', moves from passive advice to active clarity through repetition and growth.

How did Mother Design figure all this out? They staged a séance and a chakra reading, alongside an automatic drawing and writing workshop led by one of Purple Garden's holistic practitioners. It's what we've come to expect from the studio, as it encourages its team to experiment with unfamiliar disciplines. Armed with paper and charcoal, they worked instinctively, trying to ignore and bypass conscious thought. The forms and feelings that emerged from the session then became the foundation of the identity.

"So much of spiritual practice is about things you feel rather than things you can see – energy, intuition, connection and those moments when you feel altered somehow," says Joanna Tulej, creative director at Mother Design. "The challenge was how you translate that into a brand experience without falling back on the visual clichés of the category.

"We realised that if we wanted the identity to feel spontaneous and alive, we needed to allow some of that spontaneity into the way we designed it too. Starting with the séance and automatic drawing session gave us permission to step away from the screen and the rational design process. Some of the textures, energy and unexpectedness that came out of that experience found their way into the final identity."

I absolutely love this. Whatever you make of spirituality, it's nice to remember that the best work comes from being experimental and having fun. I think we need that now more than ever.

I was also fascinated to learn that the approach was partly inspired by the Swedish artist Hilma af Klint and The Five, the collective of brilliant women who experimented with trance-like states and automatic drawing to ignore conscious thought and let images and words emerge freely. (If you've not heard of her, look her up! Her paintings are considered to be among the first major abstract works in Western art history.)

But the inventiveness didn't stop at charcoal. During development, Mother Design moved back and forth between traditional design software and AI tools, iterating constantly to generate unexpected visuals and add more unpredictability to the process.

Looking at the outcome, I'll start with the typography because you can see all this play spilling through. The wordmark itself balances modern with whimsical. Elongated serifs and curved corners seem to grow outward. A "digital-first", crystal-like glyph acts as a symbol of guidance and forms the basis of the entire icon system for navigating the platform's many services.

Literatura Present and ABC Social complete the type system. Literatura's flowing descenders and serifs add to that sense of growth, and ABC Social does the functional, sensible stuff so things are easy to read on screen.

I really love the "living type" detail, inspired by cymatic patterns. That's the shape sound makes when it vibrates through a surface, if you didn't know. The subtle message is that Purple Garden helps you to grow, too.

Colour-wise, purple clearly takes the lead. But Mother Design has developed a palette of six warmer purples, deliberately moving away from the cool, blue-toned purples so common in this category. The imagery feels more intimate and tactile, while the motion reinforces the idea of growth.

The project follows Mother Design's recent rebrand of Keen, another spiritual guidance brand within the Ingenio portfolio. While Keen targeted a more mainstream, "spiritually curious" audience, Purple Garden is for those already seasoned in this space: two brands, two different jobs.

"Purple Garden is for people who already have a deep connection with spirituality and see it as an active part of how they navigate their lives," says Ariana Hellebuyck, CMO at Ingenio. "We wanted the brand to reflect the depth of the experiences our guides provide and make that connection feel just as powerful in a digital environment.

"Mother Design has created something that feels distinctive to Purple Garden and true to our audience, while giving us a richer and more immersive platform for the next stage of the brand."

The new identity is now being rolled out across Purple Garden's website, app, advertising, and social channels over the coming weeks.

It's got me thinking about how I love to switch off. Admittedly, my rituals are less mystical and don't involve any summoning. I find ironing, getting out on my bike, or spending time in the garden helps me zone out. Not as exciting, but the principle is the same. Just like Hilma, this project reminds us to play and try new things. Don't be afraid to experiment. And let creativity flow. You never know what will come out the other side.

Royal portrait painter swaps palaces for a Barnsley chippy, a Catford corner shop and a pub run by 'Slugger'

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In Coca-Cola's latest campaign, four independent business owners get something usually reserved for kings and queens: an oil-on-canvas portrait by artists who've sat opposite the late Queen, King Charles and Princess Anne.

Before the camera came along, only the very rich could afford to have themselves immortalised in oils. I'm talking royalty, aristocrats, and the odd wealthy merchant; their portraits often hang in prestigious galleries or National Trust properties. Their noble gazes in gilt frames suggest a life of privilege, while everyone else went undocumented.

Even today, a formal portrait remains a status symbol. So it's quite unusual to find a portrait of the person who serves you chips on a Saturday night. But that's what's happened in a new campaign by Coca-Cola. The drinks brand has commissioned four of Britain's finest portrait artists, each of whom has painted an official portrait of a member of the Royal Family, to paint a fish-and-chip shop owner, a café proprietor, a pub landlord and a corner shop owner. The finished works will be on show at London's Saatchi Gallery from today until 1 November, and admission is free.

It's the latest chapter in Coca-Cola's 'Localness' platform, which celebrates the people behind the pubs, shops and cafés where its drinks are served. It follows last year's documentary and photo series, The Bosses, and Counter Cultures earlier in 2026. This time around, the campaign borrows the visual language of official portraiture. And we love it. Because why shouldn't a Barnsley chippy get the same gravitas as Buckingham Palace?

Pindy Kaur by Nicky Philipps Simon Dafforn-Jones by Jamie Coreth

Everyone else seems to agree. New research commissioned by Coca-Cola Great Britain found that 79% of people think independent businesses are under-appreciated, while 80% want to see them celebrated more often. "Independent business owners create the places where local life happens, yet their contribution often goes unrecognised," says Rhona Stephen, senior director of communications for Coca-Cola Great Britain. "With Local Royalty, we wanted to celebrate them in a way that felt deliberately unexpected and enduring. By borrowing the visual language of official portraiture and placing these owners at its centre, the campaign gives their everyday impact the status it deserves."

So who got their portrait done? First up is Pindy Kaur, who took over a struggling Ocean Fish Bar in Barnsley in the 1990s. More than 25 years on, she and her family have turned it into a much-loved takeaway, known for its regulars and its family-run spirit. Pindy is a devoted admirer of the late Queen Elizabeth II, and she was painted by Nicky Philipps, who painted the Queen not once but twice.

Gino Verecchia by Gareth Reid Kaual Patel by Nick Bashall

Gino Verecchia's University Café in Glasgow was opened by his grandfather, Pasquale, in 1918, and it's been in the family for three generations. Wow. It blends Italian heritage (including a 100-year-old gelato recipe) with Scottish favourites, and it's a true Glasgow institution. It even once served Anthony Bourdain battered haggis. Gareth Reid, portrait artist to King Charles III when he was Prince of Wales, painted Gino.

Down in Dittisham, South Devon, meanwhile, The Ferry Boat Inn sits on the banks of the River Dart. Locals know it as the FBI. Brilliant. Landlord Simon Dafforn-Jones, or "Slugger" to the locals, has run it for almost 20 years, following in his dad's footsteps. He gives local teens their first jobs and champions produce from across the region, so the pub naturally sits at the heart of village life. He was painted by Jamie Coreth, who created the first official portrait of the Prince and Princess of Wales.

And finally, Kaual Patel's father bought Torridon Convenience Store in Catford, South East London, in 1984, and Kaual took over in 2000. Over the past two decades, he has made it a real community hub, stocking local producers alongside the bigger household names. His mantra is lovely: "If you're a local producer, there's a space on my shelf for you." Kaual was painted by Nick Bashall, portrait painter to Princess Anne.

Kaual Patel by Nick Bashall Gino Verecchia by Gareth Reid Pindy Kaur by Nicky Philipps Simon Dafforn-Jones by Jamie Coreth

There you have it. All worthy contenders. All beautifully depicted. Alongside their finished portraits, the Saatchi show includes behind-the-scenes footage from the sittings. It shows how each artist got to know their sitter and turned their character and history into a formal portrait.

Each owner also donates to a local charity of their choice and keeps the portrait to hang in their home or business.

MHP Group created and delivered the campaign across MHP, Mischief and Studio La Plage. Find out more on Coca-Cola's website. So next time you're enjoying a pint or picking up a chippy tea, take a moment to appreciate the people behind the counter. You might be in the presence of royalty.

Tom Hegen explores the landscapes that are paying an invisible price for clean energy

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

In his new book The Mineral Age, Tom Hegen takes aerial photographs of the mineral mines essential to our transition to low-carbon living.

Everyone knows about the climate crisis. It's almost impossible to go a day without the news reporting on record-breaking phenomena; or, if you're unlucky, you'll experience the consequences first-hand. We all know we need to do our bit to live more sustainably, whether that's by recycling or using public transport.

However, most people may be unaware of the resources enabling the world to transition to a more environmentally friendly lifestyle. These minerals, including lithium, cobalt, nickel, and copper, are part of a new frontier in humanity's quest to move away from fossil fuels. Because these rare earth elements are required for electric vehicles, batteries, and renewable energy infrastructure, demand is high.

Photographer Tom Hegen's latest project, The Mineral Age, explores where these critical minerals come from and the environmental, social, and political consequences of extracting them. Compiled over the past six years, his book reveals the often-overlooked physical footprint of technological progress.

Tom's interest in these landscapes began while photographing Germany's vast open-pit coal mines. Through this work, he became increasingly interested in humanity's quest to decarbonise its economies.

"As I researched the energy transition more deeply, I realised that moving away from fossil fuels does not mean moving away from resource extraction," he explains. "Instead, we are entering a new era of mineral dependency. Many of these resources are concentrated in just a handful of countries, creating new geopolitical dependencies and vulnerabilities"

While these minerals may ultimately lead to cleaner living, Tom reveals that their extraction often comes with significant environmental and social consequences which receive relatively little public attention. "This realisation became the starting point for The Mineral Age."

Tom's most striking insight when putting the project together was the geographical imbalance between the consumption of these critical minerals and the impacts of their extraction. "Industrialised countries in the Global North are major consumers of minerals that are often extracted in developing regions of the Global South," he says. "This imbalance is shaped by geology, economic structures, cheaper labour, and weaker environmental regulations."

He adds: "In some mining regions, environmental protections are poorly enforced, working conditions are hazardous, and local communities face serious consequences from industrial expansion. Child labour and human rights abuses also remain significant concerns in certain mining sectors."

It became increasingly clear to Tom that the environmental and social costs of technological progress are often shouldered by communities which are far removed from those who ultimately benefit from it. "We tend to celebrate the promise of clean technologies without paying much attention to the landscapes being transformed to make them possible," he says.

"In many ways, we are shifting part of the environmental burden of our consumption to other regions of the world, keeping the consequences largely out of sight." This isn't to say The Mineral Age argues against the shift away from fossil fuels, though.

"Rather, I believe we need to acknowledge that even technologies designed to create a more sustainable future have a substantial material footprint."

Tom's photographic practice is strongly research-driven, and The Mineral Age is no exception. Before travelling, he spends considerable time investigating global mineral supply chains, identifying significant mining regions, and studying the industrial processes involved.

"Accessing these places is often one of the greatest challenges," he says. "Many mining operations are extremely remote, heavily restricted, or kept out of public view. In the Democratic Republic of the Congo, for example, it took me more than two years to arrange access to some of the mining sites.

Every photograph in The Mineral Age was taken from an elevated perspective. Tom primarily works with heavy-lift drones carrying a high-resolution medium-format camera. "When drone operations are not possible, I photograph from helicopters or small aircraft. The aerial perspective allows me to reveal the immense scale of these industrial landscapes."

The Mineral Age will contain 247 aerial photographs, which Tom believes will spark curiosity, encourage conversations, and make the complex topic of energy transition more accessible. "My intention is not to provide simple answers, but to raise awareness of the often-invisible consequences of our demand for resources," he says.

"Ultimately, The Mineral Age is about the landscapes behind our modern lives, and the consequences of building the future."

Span's identity for Illinois Department of Human Rights turns a common piece of punctuation into a rallying cry

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Inspired by the posters and placards of past civil rights movements, the rebrand makes people's rights easier to see and use.

Since 1980, the Illinois Department of Human Rights (IDHR) has investigated discrimination in employment, housing, financial institutions, and public spaces based on race, sex, disability, religion, national origin, and age.

The organisation has now partnered with Chicago design studio Span on a people-centred rebrand. The aim: reinforce its commitment to protecting human rights while making those rights easier to see, understand, and use.

While IDHR aims to protect all people across Illinois, the department feared citizens could no longer see that purpose, as trust in government and public institutions has grown more fragile.

"There was a concern that the organisation was seen as rigid and bureaucratic, with its important work less visible," says Nick Adam, associate partner and design director at Span. "IDHR needed to remain neutral and fair in its work without becoming neutral about its purpose.

"And while they understood that a new logo couldn't mend an institutional divide, they knew that they could better protect people's rights by raising awareness; changing how IDHR speaks, organises information and shows up in public. After all, a right people cannot see is harder to use."

Focus on clarity

To meet this challenge, the new identity focuses on clarity to help users understand their options and feel supported.

For inspiration, the team returned to the symbols of past civil rights efforts: posters, placards, banners, and declarations designed to be seen, understood, and remembered.

The main typeface, HEX Franklin, is bold and forceful, conveying confidence, credibility and trust.

In the logo, the words "Human Rights" are emphasised to keep the department's mission front and centre, with "Illinois Department of" serving as both a descriptor and a connector.

Positioned between the two large lines of "Human Rights", it creates the visual impression of an equal sign: a simple but meaningful gesture towards fairness, reciprocity and balance under the law.

A sign of equality

"The equal sign gave us a way to make the idea of human rights visible before a single word is read," says Adam. "Equality belongs to everyone."

He adds that the lines echo both flag stripes and the graphic language of designers like Chicago's Lester Beall, celebrated for his work for the Rural Electrification Administration.

Social and rapid-response communications have been built as a deployment kit, with established ways to handle announcements, photography, rights information, events and urgent messages.

Public events required another ready-to-use system of banners, flags, step-and-repeats, table coverings, signage, presentations and printed materials.

This has helped IDHR put rights information directly into people's hands at Juneteenth events and the Illinois State Fair. At Chicago's Pride Parade, it allowed the department to create and distribute stickers, buttons, apparel and other materials to the crowd.

Span has a longstanding relationship with IDHR, having previously helped launch the statewide support service Help Stop Hate. And Adam stresses that this project has been built for the long haul, dubbing it: "Conviction without propaganda. That feels especially important in design right now. And as Illinois locals, we can see and feel the impact of our own work in real time."

IDHR director Jim Bennett is delighted with the result. "Our goal was to create a brand that people could recognise, connect with and immediately know our agency exists to protect their rights," he says. "Every Illinoisan should know they are protected under state law, regardless of who they are, who they love, or where they come from."

When I started out designing for the web, we would create designs at a fixed 1024 pixels wide. It was considered just about a safe width that could be displayed in most web browsers on most computer screens without having to scroll horizontally. If the screen was a lot wider than 1024 pixels, we centered our design in the middle of the page. We looked back at the previous era where people were creating designs at 800 pixels wide and luxuriated in the space we had available to us.

The Apple website in 2006 was designed to fit inside 800px, and mostly made up of images of text.

This approach to web design worked well for people like me coming from a more traditional graphic design background. We were used to designing for print, where a printed poster, booklet, CD cover, or book, always had a fixed height and width. We had absolute control over every millimeter of our design and how the final product would look. And so we took our paper-based print design precision into a pixel-precise web design approach (sometimes known as imperative design). 

Fallbacks and progressive enhancement

On the development side, we had fallbacks and progressive enhancement as best practices we could use when that pixel-precision didn’t quite work. These were often necessary as each browser could render a web page in wildly different ways, even more so across different browser versions, and browsers didn’t auto-update like they do now.

When the content contained varying lengths of text, we could use scrollable areas. When an image didn’t load, the user would get the alternative text. (If someone had bothered writing it!) If the chosen “web-safe” font wasn’t on the user’s device, we could fallback to the system font, or use a font family stack to specify similar fonts that were an acceptable substitution.

Progressively enhancing a box with border-radius

We could start using new features available to us in CSS like border-radius for rounded corners (circa 2015), but we would use progressive enhancement; a box could have an acceptable default of square corners for most people, and those whose browsers supported rounded corners would get the curvy version.

Providing alternative design options meant we gave up a little bit of control over how the final product looked, but we increased the chances that it wouldn’t be broken, and that the person using that final product had a good experience.

Even though these fallbacks and progressive enhancements were design decisions, their use and execution was mostly the developers’ responsibility as they involved code. If you were lucky, designers and developers could work out a solution together.

Eventually 1024 pixels wasn’t enough anymore. The mobile web meant that people could get a version of our sites with web browsers on their phones. Then came smartphones, then apps. As UI designers, we needed to be able to design for flexibility.

Flexible and responsive design

In modern UI design, we’ve accepted that we are rarely faced with fixed content and fixed device sizes. We are usually designing for a moving target (sometimes literally): web browsers that can render at any size on any device, UIs that need to work in multiple languages which have different character sizes and word lengths, or templates for content that’ll have different text and media every time it’s used. And we don’t have the time to design a different version for every possible combination.

Responsive design started a movement of designing for the built-in flexibility of the web. Instead of designs using predefined fixed sizes, responsive design responds to the width of the browser viewport, using media queries for breakpoints (where the design “breaks”) to define constraints. We could decide to make a menu vertical on a narrow mobile-width viewport, and horizontal on a wide desktop-width viewport. We could choose a single-column layout for narrow viewports and a multi-column layout for wider viewports.

We usually stack content on narrower viewports and make more of the horizontal space on wider viewports.

While breakpoint-based designs were more flexible than our fixed-width creations, many of us ended up designing different layouts for the most popular “mobile, tablet, desktop” breakpoints without putting much thought into where a design might break in between. It was hard to visualize and test these in-between points using a design platform.

Again, developers often end up making the design decisions here, working out the constraints at the stage of implementing the design in the browser. And, again, if you have a good designer-developer relationship, it might be a collaborative process.

In between breakpoints can make for a poor use of the available space.

But, more often than not, our final products end up broken between breakpoint sizes. You can see this on many of your favorite sites and apps! Sometimes there’s weird empty spaces, images that are way too big, content that just looks a little wrong, all because you’ve decided to browse at a slightly unusual viewport size: mobile in landscape, tablet in portrait, split screen on a laptop.

There is a way to make this easier!

What is declarative design?

Nowadays, responsive design has evolved thanks to intrinsic and declarative design.

Jen Simmons originated intrinsic web design in 2018, focusing on the CSS layout features that were arriving in browsers around that time. Flexbox and Grid layouts (these might sound familiar if you use Penpot!) gave us more control over a flexible layout, and ways to combine different layout types for complex and sophisticated structures.

One of Jen Simmons’ many early demos of CSS Grid.

CSS Flexbox provided layouts that could grow and shrink depending on their container and contents. CSS Grid provided two-dimensional layouts where you could control columns and rows together. Both types of layouts could bring together fixed and flexible elements simultaneously, as well as new flexible ways to size things: the fr unit, the auto keyword, and functions like minmax() and clamp().

Designing for the layout features that are intrinsic (or native) to the browser, means you can make the most of what the browser is good at, like complex layout logic and calculations. 

With a fixed design, you’d have to work out that your three column layout needs 3x columns that are 301.3333 recurring pixels wide if it’s going to have 40px padding on each side, 20px spacing between each column, and still fit inside 1024px. And that only works in a viewport that’s 1024px wide. 

In intrinsic design, you say you want 3x columns of 1fr wide with 40px padding, 20px column gaps, and that’ll work in most viewport sizes. You could even switch out the fixed pixel sizes for the simple vw (viewport width) unit. Then 40px padding becomes ~4vw and column gap becomes ~2vw, adapting depending on the width of the viewport. You could use your vw value as the default inside a clamp function to specify minimum and maximum values, so your padding and gaps don’t become too tight or too roomy.

In 2022, Jeremy Keith built on intrinsic design when he came up with declarative design. Declarative design means creating the rules your design should follow, instead of deciding exactly how it looks in every situation. Instead of saying the column must always be 301.3333px wide, you say the column should take up 1fr of the space, no matter how wide that is.

Not having to do every design for 2+ viewport sizes… that’s a lot simpler, right?!

You design layouts through rules and constraints rather than describing the pixel value of each property of each element in painstaking detail, or specifying new and different values for every single breakpoint.

Every Layout by Heydon Pickering and Andy Bell is the ultimate resource for doing declarative design through “algorithmic layout design.”

And while these layouts might seem complicated compared to arranging and aligning elements using X and Y coordinates, they give you the ultimate combination of flexibility and control. You can assume every element is flexible by default, and then decide where you need to impose control.

Knowing your medium

Declarative design, like responsive design, does require a basic understanding of what’s possible in the web browser. But that doesn’t mean you have to be able to code like a developer. I like to think of it as knowing your medium, in the same way that a book designer understands how different paper weights affect the print, and how colors translate from screen to ink. 

Knowing our medium as UI designers is understanding the elements available to us, their native behaviors and customizable properties. The more you know about your medium, the more you can take advantage of its built-in features and benefits. As Andy Bell puts it, “Be the browser’s mentor, not its micromanager.”

Fixed design vs responsive design vs declarative design

I feel like these are tricky concepts to get across, so here’s one final example comparing the different design approaches for a navigation menu layout:

  • Fixed design: The navigation menu has a width of 390px. It is centered horizontally on the page using margin: auto. If you change the number of links in the navigation, you have to adjust the width of the navigation container or the links will be cut off when they exceed the width of the container. You can’t have more links than will fit into that 390px.
  • Responsive design: The navigation menu has a width of 200px for 599px wide viewports or narrower, a width of 400px for 600px wide viewports or wider. It is centered horizontally using margin: auto. The links are floated, so they reflow to a second row if they exceed the width of the navigation container, or you can change the width of the navigation at that viewport size to create a better flow.
  • Declarative design: The navigation menu is set to fit the width of its contents using flexbox. It is centered horizontally on the page using flexbox. The links are set to flex-wrap so if they exceed the width of the navigation container, they reflow to a second and third row. The navigation automatically fits on every viewport size, no numbers or calculations needed, just a description of the design intent.

Try this demo yourself on Codepen.

Declarative design frees you from the unattainable ideal of pixel-precise control (“imperative design”) over a layout. It was always impossible to translate fixed pixel layouts precisely into code for an inherently flexible browser viewport. Now you can design layouts with these specific yet flexible instructions that align perfectly with code. In fact, in Penpot, what you see in the Design panel is reflected in the Inspect tab, so all the terminology is the same for both designers and developers. 

Declarative design is the future

As UI designers, the formats for our final products are going to keep expanding, evolving and changing, faster than we can possibly keep up. VR headsets, car dashboards, smart watches and glasses are just the beginning. It’s impossible to design for this many formats and that’s before we even get to the development stage. As Pablo Ruiz-Múzquiz, Penpot’s CEO, wrote in his article on Does Declarative Design foster superior collaboration with developers?:

Developers spend too much time figuring out the inherent behavior rules that stem from a particular design. This is where a lot of design translation gets lost. By adopting a Declarative approach to design, designers can help teams speed up the development cycle.

And it’s not only that creating declarative designs that can adapt to, and accommodate, their destination makes our work easier. Designing accessible, consistent, user-friendly experiences makes our products more usable and successful with the end user. Everybody wins!

César Cid turns famous artists into letters in his playful alphabet series

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The creative lead at CESS has created a fun new Artphabet featuring 26 notable artists.

It's a testament to an artist's talent that their paintings can be abstracted and remain recognisable. César Cid proves this point 26 times over in The Artphabet, a 3D typographic series that turns famous artworks into letters matching their creators' names. And it all begins with an A-shaped Campbell's soup can inspired by Andy Warhol himself.

The Artphabet is a personal project for César, who works as the creative lead at the Madrid-based studio CESS by day. Inspired by the annual typography challenge 36 Days of Type, he wanted to bring his love of art and his 3D work together in a series that could explore different visual worlds.

Rather than simply wrapping a famous artwork around typography, César wanted to imagine how an artwork's colours, shapes and materials could become a letter in three dimensions. D from the series is a perfect example, as it adapts the shark and formaldehyde tank from Damien Hirst's 1991 installation The Physical Impossibility of Death in the Mind of Someone Living.

Elsewhere, Banksy's Girl with Balloon becomes the basis for the letter B, and the swirling pastoral imagery from Van Gogh's Starry Night inspires the letter V. Given the nature of the alphabet, though, creative liberties were taken with certain letters.

"My initial idea was for each letter to match the artist's initial, but that wasn't possible across the whole alphabet," says César, referring to how the terrified figure from Edvard Munch's The Scream becomes zig-zagged into the letter Z. "The process became a way for me to learn more about art, to look more closely at each artist's work and understand what makes their style distinctive."

Once the full range of 26 artworks was completed, The Artphabet soon found an international audience. This encouragement prompted César to release the images in other formats, such as a popular collection of physical prints. "I love seeing people choose these pieces for their homes, and connecting with the project beyond the screen has been especially rewarding," he says.

The Artphabet has already grown beyond the original digital project, and a "substantial number" of canvas prints are being bought through private sales and online auctions, but César isn't done with it yet.

"A book of The Artphabet will be published early next year, which is a big milestone for the project," he says.

"I'm also in ongoing conversations about expanding The Artphabet into other formats, and I'm always open to new collaborations with publishers, brands and cultural organisations. I'd love to keep finding new ways for people to discover and enjoy the collection."

The best new typefaces for October 2026

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

From an industrial slab named after a hardness scale to a display face woven from memories of lace, this October brings a bumper crop of new typefaces.

Is it that time already? The clocks have gone back, the weather is getting colder and wetter, and the countdown to 25 December has begun in earnest. But for designers looking for exciting new fonts, it might feel like Christmas has come a couple of months early. October has delivered one of the most eclectic typographic hauls of the year.

We're talking a variable font five years in the making, a humanist sans designed for dyslexic readers, a display typeface assembled from clay impressions of gravestones, and a geometric stencil that can switch between sharp and rounded at the pull of a variable axis. Elsewhere there's elegance in a new revival of a 1921 Garamond interpretation, a sense of solidity in an eight-weight industrial slab named after a metallurgical hardness test, and outright quirkiness in a type system that deliberately sits "at the threshold between reading and looking".

In short, while it's easy to feel design and creativity are being taken over by AI, the fresh typefaces we've collected below remain very much hand-crafted. That thought alone should keep you warm and cosy as the nights draw in.

1. Twista Sans by Viktor Nübel

Twista Sans is something different: a uniwidth typeface. That's not the same as being monospaced: Twista Sans keeps characters proportional. But all its glyphs share the same horizontal width, so you can switch weights without your layout reflowing, opening up new possibilities for hover effects and UI animations. Designed as a sans companion to Nübel's display face Twista, Twista Sans could work well for packaging, branding and editorial.

2. Corsario by Felix Braden

Cologne-based Felix Braden, the designer behind Floodfonts, has channelled five years of work into Corsario. Inspired by the British transitional style, it features sharp, prominent serifs that make text stand out. With 54 styles, this variable font family lets you set both elegant headlines and legible captions from a single font file.

3. Triode by MuirMcNeil

MuirMcNeil's work sits somewhere between typeface design and visual art, and Triode is a prime example. This new geometric type system explores what happens at the crossroads between language and pattern. Its precisely calibrated components can be combined, layered, and offset to create transitions and collisions between language, image, pattern, and texture. To mark the release, MuirMcNeil has published an edition of 40 unique colour posters, each measuring 50 × 70 cm.

4. Kellarie by Laura Worthington

When you need a script with real warmth that feels hand-drawn, Laura Worthington is usually worth checking out. Compact lowercase forms, buoyant rhythm, and a lively forward slant give words the momentum of hand lettering, while sweeping capitals, generous loops, and curling flourishes add personality. This font works well on packaging, stationery, and editorial headers.

5. Thorn Serif by Type Forward

Type Forward has an interesting catalogue, and Thorn Serif is a confident new addition. It's inspired by the raw, distinctive lettering on ancient Roman coins. The family takes the compact proportions, heavy serifs, and low-contrast construction of these historical forms and translates them into a contemporary type system.

6. Bw Ubica by Branding with Type

Branding with Type is known for sans-serifs that are clean without being anonymous, and Bw Ubica continues in that tradition. Its sharp geometry and balanced anatomy provide impact, while its balanced x-height and slightly condensed proportions are optimised for high legibility without losing a single hint of its character. Available in 12 styles, this font gives brands a unified voice across editorial layouts, user interfaces, and physical product sleeves.

7. Hyperspace Cargo by Swell Type

Hyperspace Cargo looks exactly like its name. Derived from the earlier Hyperspace Race, it's an all-caps, stencil typeface available in a range of weights from Light to Heavy. Horizontal-line Stylistic Alternates add further variety, and it also supports Cyrillic, Greek and Vietnamese. The variable version offers Weight and Roundness axes for extra flexibility.

8. Mourn by Nina Type

Mexican type designer Ubaldo Ortiz spent two years building this display face around the structural logic of bobbin lace, painstakingly constructing each character letter by letter before weaving lace patterns around them. Mourn is the launch typeface for his new studio Nina Type, which also offers the sharp serif Osé and everyday workhorse Nina Sans.

9. Pen by DDOTT × Morula

What happens when a pen pauses on paper? Those small drops of ink at the moment of hesitation, correction, or interruption form the core concept of Pen, a new typeface from DDOTT, developed in collaboration with Morula. Designer Dominik Thieme launched the project with a live plotting performance in Barcelona, and the release includes a thoughtful essay tracing the history of automated drawing and writing.

10. CoFo Cinema1909 by Contrast Foundry

Cinema1909 began as a custom typeface for one of the world's oldest cinemas, inspired by metro-station lettering, before being released as a retail typeface. This month, Contrast Foundry published a significant update, adding lowercase letters, ordinals and expanded language support. Combining elegance and decorative details with a refined feel, it works particularly well in tight, compact headlines.

11. WTF Hammer by W Type Foundry

W Type Foundry built Hammer around a single premise: what can you do with proportion alone? The answer seems to be: well, quite a lot, actually. This contemporary grotesk superfamily features 12 width variants, plus matching italics. Moving between styles creates rhythm and contrast, making headlines and layouts feel dynamic, not static.

12. GS Merca by GS Type

Gerard Sierra has spent a lot of time with the ornate lettering on the façade of the 1920s Art Nouveau market in the Spanish city of Tarragona. The font this inspired carries its source material's character while adding a stencil-like, rebellious feel. The lowercase expands this language, creating a typeface with enough identity to shift between the vernacular and sharper, more contemporary contexts.

13. Altus Grotesk by Type Innovations

Alex Kaczun, former principal type designer at Linotype-Hell, helped shape the Adobe PostScript Font Library, and his solo foundry, Type Innovations, continues to build on that legacy. Altus Grotesk is a high-impact geometric display font that combines sharp-cornered, highly stylised capitals with clean circular forms and generous proportions. A strong choice for logos, posters, and headline branding work where you need both presence and clarity.

14. IvyMazarin Big by Ivy Foundry

IvyMazarin demonstrates the difference between reviving a historical typeface and simply reproducing it. Designer Jan Maack took Astrée, a 1921 Garamond interpretation commissioned for the Imprimerie Nationale in Paris, and drew out a display version with precision and sensitivity. Think dramatic contrast, generous proportions and a sense of grandeur.

15. Thalo by Signal Type Foundry

Thalo began as a commission from a state transportation agency: a humanist sans designed to meet accessibility requirements for signage and timetables. Max Phillips at Signal Type Foundry in Dublin has kept contrast low, with generous letterspacing and open counters and apertures throughout. Letters such as b, d, p, and q have been carefully drawn to minimise confusion for dyslexic readers, while I, l, 1, and i are as distinct as possible. This clarity means Thalo can create impact in branding and editorial, as well as signage.

16. Shuffle by Local Type

Originally designed for a children's healthcare centre in Brazil, Shuffle is a two-axis variable display font (weight and bounce) that ranges from bold and exuberant to lighter and sharper, with 12 static styles too. It's the first release from Local Type, João Luiz Wittkowski's new foundry based in Valencia, Spain, and one style is free for personal and commercial use.

17. Blubird by Rajesh Kumar

Blubird is a contemporary narrow sans-serif with nine weights, 36 styles, and two optical sizes (Display and Text) for genuine range. With dedicated italics, this font strikes a strong balance between visual impact and typographic refinement.

18. Vestige by Claire Wilde

Vestige takes the idea of a handmade typeface into new, unusual territory. Designer Claire Wilde developed this uppercase display typeface from physical clay impressions of lettering found on gravestones, while retaining the irregularities and weathered character of the original impressions. Her documentation of the process has reached 147k views.

19. Puerile Grotesque by Applied Systems Design Studio

Alister Shapley at Applied Systems Design Studio has been thinking about what "play" actually means in typography, and Puerile Grotesque is his answer. While his earlier font, Puerile Display, explored ornament and expressive forms, Puerile Grotesque questions the underlying structure of letterforms. Together, they form a complementary, convention-busting family.

20. Magmæ by Arillatype

Pedro Arilla and Cristina Villellas at Arillatype named their new industrial typeface after the molten rock beneath the Earth's crust. It's a design of opposites: fluid, organic curves contrast with a mechanical digital skeleton; soft outer contours meet structured, squared counters. Engineered for high functionality and precise performance, this font has a raw energy that makes it a strong choice for packaging, industrial branding, and architecture-based projects.

21. Scooch by Fred Wiltshire

Scooch is a large serif family that pairs gaiety with typographic rigour – no mean feat. The swash capitals, influenced by 19th-century display typefaces, add flourish; the lowercase is tamed by comparison, with a simplified "t" that runs through the family as a visual thread. One of those rare display families that can function as text when needed.

22. Clamp by Daniel Iglesias

Clamp is a type system that builds multiple personalities directly into a single typeface, rather than creating separate styles. Designers can adapt it to different contexts by changing its character through OpenType alternates.

23. Brinell by Latinotype

Latinotype named its new slab serif after the Brinell hardness scale, a 1900 engineering test that measures a material's resistance to deformation. The team drew on classic industrial slabs like Rockwell and updated the proportions and details by removing some serifs, flattening contrast, and creating a cleaner overall look. Its intentional inconsistencies (the terminal on the /a, the slightly off-rule details) give it a warmth that precise, corrected type often lacks.

The Roundup: Lessons on typography from Tom Foley, Ellen Lupton, and Jonathon Hoefler

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

I began my career in physical product design, and apart from a few introductory studio design classes I took while studying product design as an undergrad (in what was technically an engineering major), I didn’t have much grounding in typography or graphic design.

Over the years I got more interested in it, and picked up books like Ellen Lupton’s Graphic Design: The New Basics. But it wasn’t until I decided to start an underwater photography magazine with a friend that I challenged myself to approach it more professionally.

By the time we went to print with the first issue, I was confident that the results were, as Larry David might say, pretty, pretty good. How wrong I was.

The first issue of Wetpixel Quarterly. The cover design was OK (although I didn’t realize the UPC code could be on the back!) but don’t look too closely at the typography inside 🙈

After sharing the print version with a friend who had studied graphic design in college,1 he pointed out several fundamental errors I’d made in typography and layout. First of all, the text across the columns wasn’t aligned to the grid. There were several other noob mistakes, but the one that, in retrospect, was the biggest missed opportunity was my choice of typefaces.

I ended up using what were essentially some of the default options in Adobe’s InDesign (Minion Pro and Myriad Pro). Not terrible, but not especially unique or creative either. Perhaps the equivalent of the non-offensive invitation your great aunt might send out for her dog’s 15th birthday. By the final issue, the design and layout had gotten better, but I’m sure there are still plenty of mistakes that better designers than me would point out.

In later years, as I continued to do graphic design work for clients both in print and on the web, I learned about resources like Jonathan Hoefler’s site (now Typography.com), which offered lovely type pairings.

Fast forward another few years, and we’ve been lucky enough to have some of these typography legends on Design Better: Ellen Lupton, Jonathan Hoefler, and most recently, Tom Foley.

In this issue of The Roundup, we’ll explore what we learned from these experts, and what you might apply to your own design practice, whether you’re a beginner, intermediate, or expert.

1. Pair by structure first, personality second

Most of us pair type by what we’ve seen before—a vibe we liked: I dig how this designer paired this serif with this sans. Tom’s advice is to ignore the vibe for a minute and look at the skeleton.

“There’s some really good principles that can make it a lot easier,” he told us. “And they’re actually looking at less expressive stuff and more structural things.”

One test is ancestry—tracing each typeface back to the tools that shaped it:

“Say you’ve got a serif headline typeface and it’s a modern or a Bodoni style, so has…high contrast, and the shoulders blend into the stems and it has fine serifs. That typeface model, that modern model, eventually evolved into grotesk typefaces… So those typefaces always pair better with grotesques because they have the same structure.”

Mid-century designers like Josef Müller-Brockmann used grotesk typefaces extensively in their work.

In other words, ask “what structural aspects of this typeface have come from which calligraphic tradition? Broad nib or pointed nib.” For a deep dive on that idea, Gerrit Noordzij’s The Stroke: Theory of Writing (1985) is the source of the “translation versus expansion” framework Tom alluded to.
