Rooftop lounges are mainstays of the Miami skyline offering open-air escapes above the bustle of the city. For the first U.S. outpost of Japanese restaurant Mimi Kakushi, London-based design firm Pirajean Lees brings together the vibrancy of Miami, the glamour of 1920s Osaka, and the cinematic allure of old Hollywood.

Photography by Robert Reiger

Clémence Pirajean and James Lees, who also designed the restaurant’s Dubai location, drew inspiration for the Miami interiors from the Japanese silent film actor Sessue Hayakawa.

Located on the fourth floor of the Delano Miami hotel, the indoor-outdoor restaurant takes cues from an imagined life for the actor, envisioned as a world traveler whose surroundings reflect different sides of his personality.

Photography by Andrea Di Lorenzo

That fictional narrative takes shape through a mix of period-inspired details and open-air spaces that connect the restaurant to its coastal setting. The terrace accommodates different dining experiences, from casual gatherings to more intimate meals, and is reserved for hotel guests and Delano Members Club members.

Photography by Robert Reiger

The open sushi counter places food preparation at the center of the dining experience, giving guests a view of head chef Henry Park’s team at work. A DJ station sets the rhythm throughout the day and evening service, while the bar takes an unconventional approach to presenting its cocktails. Instead of a list of signature cocktails printed on paper, patrons are handed a vintage View-Master to click through reels of drinks featuring botanicals associated with East Asian flavors.

Photography by Robert Reiger

Lush greenery strategically placed screens divide the terrace into smaller seating areas, providing a sense of enclosure while maintaining ocean views. Counter seating and upholstered banquettes accommodate different ways of dining, whether it’s full-course meals or a selection of shared plates of sashimi and tempura.

Sunlight filters through timber bead curtains and stained glass, casting shifting patterns across a palette of warm browns and creams. Fringed throw pillows add texture, while hand-painted landscapes and custom furniture introduce references to traditional craftsmanship.

Photography by Robert Reiger

Together, the layered materials, shifting daylight, music, and open-air setting create an atmosphere that changes throughout the day. The result is a restaurant that draws on the theatricality of its cinematic inspiration while remaining connected to Miami Beach.

Perched above 41 acres of rugged Wyoming terrain, a cantilevered, boomerang-shaped residence folds into the mountainside. Designed by CLB Architects, the home makes an unforgettable impression, a quiet marvel of restraint and precision framed by sweeping vistas.

Photography by Matthew Millman

Spanning 7,000 square feet, the Boomerang Residence serves as the owners’ contemporary holiday home. Conceptualized as a simple, broken line, the boomerang-shaped plan wraps around the hillside to create a central courtyard. The main level sits above a concrete plinth, establishing a dialogue between the drama of the expansive views and a quieter, inward-facing focus.

Photography by Matthew Millman

This sense of duality inspired the home’s design. “We were tasked with maximizing the potential of this site, while still sitting lightly on the land,” says Eric Logan, partner at CLB Architects. “The form of the building allowed us to create apertures inside for experiences and outward-facing views.”

Photo by Matthew Millman

Arranged across two levels, the floor plan establishes a natural division between public and private spaces, with circulation playing a key role in the overall layout. At the heart of the home, the main level’s kitchen and dining area serve as the epicenter of family activity.

RELATED READING: CLB Designs a Multigenerational Mountain Home in Wyoming

Off the garage, a ski room features a floating fireplace and built-in seating. The lower level accommodates guest suites, an infinity pool, and a gym with views toward one of North America’s highest peaks, providing a home base for the family’s active, outdoor lifestyle.

Photo by Matthew Millman

Integral to the design, the residence incorporates an energy-efficient air-source heat pump system that uses 30% less energy than conventional heating and cooling systems. Hydronic flooring and a highly insulated building envelope, complete with triple-paned windows, further enhance thermal regulation and reduce energy costs.

Photography by Matthew Millman

Rooted in the rugged terrain, the material palette creates a tactile conversation between earth and architecture. “There’s a deliberate experience as you circulate through the house, which centers on the common materials of wood, steel, and concrete,” says Bryan James, principal at CLB. “It’s like a modern chalet.”

Photography by Matthew Millman

Douglas fir flooring and ceilings, treated with a soft whitewash, bring a sense of calm to the contrasting blackened steel and board-formed concrete elements. Together, the organic palette and abundant natural light create a sense of depth that shifts throughout the day.

Photography by Matthew Millman

With interiors that feel akin to a living gallery, every detail has been carefully considered, allowing the residence to feel both unique and deeply connected to the client. A dichroic sculpture by Soo Sunny hangs suspended over the central stair, while a 20-foot-long island snakes its way through the kitchen.

Photography by Matthew Millman

For Sarah Kennedy, principal and interior design director at CLB Architects, the palette was intentionally understated. “The goal was to let the architectural materials be very quiet and ramp up the interior experience with artful furnishings.”

Photography by Matthew Millman

A refined sanctuary, the Boomerang Residence is as dynamic as the land itself. Viewed from across the valley, its shadowed wood exterior blends into the landscape. For the team at CLB, this was the goal.

“The home is doing its best to keep its head down and let the natural context be the star,” Logan says.

Photography by Matthew Millman

Tom Foley: How to pair typefaces and why the best brand fonts go unnoticed

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Tom Foley didn’t set out to design typefaces. As a postgraduate student at Central Saint Martins in London, he was researching why Ireland, unlike Switzerland, the Netherlands, or Germany, never seemed to have a confident typographic voice of its own.

The answer led him through centuries of colonial history, to a so-called “Irish type” invented during the Reformation to make printed scripture look the way outsiders thought Irish readers would want it to look, and back further still, to fifth-century monks who may have given us the first true lowercase letters and the word space. The best response to that research, Tom realized, was a typeface. He called it Nib, and it now lives on Dublin’s heritage plaques.

That mix of deep research and hands-on craft has defined his career since. Tom worked on the first phase of Netflix Sans, a typeface you may see every day and probably never notice. At Monotype, he rose to Executive Creative Director and led custom work like the National Portrait Gallery’s contemporary serif. This year he walked away from that role to become Creative Type Director at Studio DRAMA, because he wanted to get back to making type.

We talk about how cultural context shapes letterforms, how to balance heritage with something that feels modern, and Tom’s practical framework for pairing typefaces, which starts with calligraphic roots and vertical metrics rather than vibes. We also get into why he left a prestigious executive role, how a childhood struggle with reading may have sharpened his eye for letters, and the books and typefaces he thinks every designer should know.

Bio

Tom Foley is Creative Type Director at Studio DRAMA in London, where he designs custom typefaces for brands. He was previously Executive Creative Director at Monotype, where his work included a custom serif for the National Portrait Gallery and his typeface Cotford. Before that, he spent years at Dalton Maag, working on multi-script projects for Nokia and the first phase of Netflix Sans. Tom came to type design through research: his postgraduate thesis at Central Saint Martins on Irish typographic history produced Nib, the typeface now used on Dublin City Council’s heritage plaques.

Short version: Tom Foley is Creative Type Director at Studio DRAMA in London and former Executive Creative Director at Monotype. His work spans custom typefaces for Netflix, Nokia, and the National Portrait Gallery.

The day of small things

📅 Aug 15, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

I spotted this quote by Shirley Hibberd on the side of an enormous glass case, just outside the departures gate at SFO:

Let us not despise the day of small things. A windowsill garden is better than no garden, and the dark area where cats quarrel and damp makes patches of unsightly mould, may be made bright with healthy vegetation that shall be worth a little care and very much love.

This lovely quote is from Shirley’s book, published in 1870, called Rustic Adornments for Homes of Taste and just look at this punk thing. For all the lovely feel-good-vibes and gardening advice of that quote above, it’s clear that this book is a cursed object that beckons from beyond our realm. Although, making a gardening book exclusively for goths like me is kinda neat.

Anyway, Shirley’s quote, the glass case, and the whole exhibit at SFO is called Indoor Eden: Victorian Jardinières and I feel like it was setup exclusively to ruin my day. The exhibit is a celebration of art nouveau Victorian pottery and I can’t think of much else in life that we should be actively rooting against: stumpy dark green leaves fold over ceramic fish with their mouths wide open; an ugly brown vase stands proud with a few ugly dandelions haphazardly carved into it; a psychedelic, gaping-wound-red butterfly has been stamped onto a tall pot that’s perched on an equally upsetting, even taller pot.

It’s all just profoundly ugly. Overly complicated. Frilly. The colors are wrong. All the pots on display beg for attention in a way that feels embarrassing, reminding me of stuffy rooms in England with silverware locked in cabinets and nice china brought out for special occasions. Or it reminds me of pictures of the Queen printed on tacky plates and all the weird romanticism in England about what the Victorian era was, and what the Empire did. Each piece in these glass cases fail so profoundly and in such remarkable ways that it draws me in, pulls me close. For a while I forget where I am, that I’m at the airport to say goodbye to my father in-law, and instead I’m locked in an eternal death stare with these ugly 150 year old pots.

I know, I know. This meanness is unpleasant and these ugly things are expertly made and technically competent beyond anything I could ever manage with my enormous sausage fingers and terrible hand-eye coordination. A lot of care and attention, decades of experience, was poured into making these objects. But a bad song or a bad movie or a bad anything is still bad, despite the effort made.

Here’s how I excuse myself when being a grump.

When I complain I’m honoring the effort of whoever made these things by trying to understand why I hate them. It’s genuinely interesting to me! A lot of folks dismiss their hatred or are mean about what they dislike in boastful ways but I want to understand why and how these things suck. I want to learn more about them in the hope that I can avoid making anything like it. It’s useful information about myself, like knowing that I’m never going to enjoy karaoke. It’s just too late, okay? I’m thirty six years old and I’ve spent enough time pretending to have a good time singing Shania Twain. It’s ok to hate karaoke! Let me live!

Back at the airport, I chuckle to myself.

Shirley is trying to be nice. In his book and in that quote he’s trying to remind me that great things can come from small problems. That all I have to do is tidy up, clean up a garden, remove a weed, or do small things for the sake of small things. But here I am—a total grump—despising the smallness of these acts. I want to yell at Shirley: no, actually, a windowsill is not better than a garden goddammit. A damp patch in your house is gross and instead of fixing it you should run away, get a new apartment, burn the place to the ground. Small, ugly things should be unacceptable. Fix this minor inconvenience by changing everything about your life and move a thousand miles away, change your name, forget everyone you love, adopt a whole new identity.

And please, for the love of all that his holy in this world, stop making these goddamn Victorian Jardinières.

(Ahem. Sorry, Shirley).

📅 Aug 03, 2020  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

When is tricky these days, but it’s the only question my expectant monkey mind can ask. Time feels especially squishy this year, as my daily schedule drifts further and further away from the traditional 9-5 work window and months slip by and weeks drag on. It’s been hard to get my arms around time, but my best estimate is that I do this thing every other week or so, and I want to tell you about it.

Whenever I When? myself into agitation; whenever an iffy day gets its claws into me in a previously unimaginable 2020-ish way; whenever I find myself pausing on walks wanting to knock the phones out of people’s hands; whenever it’s a gloomy November in my soul and I have to pause for a moment to remember the month, I escape to the sofa to cleanse myself with a couple hours of what I have endearingly named Hands TV.

Black gloves, screwdrivers, wrenches, rotary grinders, isopropyl alcohol, sandblasters, and toothbrushes… all working on chainsaws, toy trucks, unidentified military components, and Game Boys. Time-lapse restoration videos have become my escape from 2020. I fire up the YouTube app and sit through hours of dudes scrubbing and grinding on old busted junk until my shadow lifts and progress, justice, peace, safety, and a smile feel possible again.

I began watching Hands TV out of curiosity. An interest in Japanese woodcut prints led me to videos about Japanese joinery and hand tools. The algorithm extended this logical path into a delirious wish for decontextualized restoration videos of Egyptian daggers, and off I went down the autoplay road. It’s been true love ever since.

The rules of the genre are straight-forward:

  • Only restore items that fit on a workbench
  • The first and last shot must always be your hands placing the object on the workbench
  • No talking, no music
  • No faces, only hands
  • Wear gloves most of the time
  • Jump cuts galore
  • After disassembly, Knoll all the parts together for one hero shot
  • Sanding and grinding sequences should be sped up to 2-3× (you eventually acclimate to the sounds that resemble dental work)
  • Use time-lapse footage when needed, especially when soaking parts in rust remover or ultrasonic cleaning solution
  • Tersely label what you’re doing with text in the corner
  • Always provide a link to your screwdriver kit and merch
  • Chekov’s blowtorch (if you see a blowtorch in any shot, it will be used)

Hands TV has become a way to fill time while stuck in my quarantine haze, an unlikely antidote to media-induced despair, and a counterpoint to doomscrolling. It is care as entertainment: a fulfilled wish to watch something come together instead of fall apart, and an opportunity to witness a reversal of neglect with low emotional and cognitive overhead. This perspective is a tiny bit treacly and very much an overreach, but it’s been a tough year, I’m exhausted, and tri-hex screwdrivers now get me emotional.
