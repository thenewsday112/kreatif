Alice Bucknell’s The Alluvials

 

In 2023, artist Alice Bucknell released The Alluvials, a video work filled with wolves, moths, and sediment that looks at a version of Los Angeles in the near future. In this California city, the sand that had once been characteristic of its beaches has taken over downtown, effectively turning the sprawl of highways and skyscrapers into a desert of dunes and dust. Through this speculative lens on an alternate reality, Bucknell presents both the history and consequences of this dried-up world. The work examines the history of the Tongva people of the Greater Los Angeles Basin while also imagining the water desalination corporation Qwench, a perpetually burning landscape, and an eternity in which the sky remains in its golden hour.

 

A year later, the project evolved into The Alluvials video game (2024), a multilevel game that explores the world inside the film. With each embodied exploration of its digital realm, the project presents the uncanny and nearly incomprehensible fallout of climate change. In their exploration of a not-so-far-off Los Angeles, Bucknell poses the questions: Is this how we will want to live? And if the answer is no, what modes of action do we have access to in order to reroute our course in the progression of climate change?

Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video work, 2023 | all images courtesy the artist unless stated otherwise Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video work, 2023 | all images courtesy the artist unless stated otherwise

 

 

Embodied perspectives of NPCs

 

The word alluvial means of or relating to alluvium, which Merriam-Webster describes as ‘clay, silt, sand, gravel, or similar detrital material deposited by running water.’ In the game, Bucknell puts players in a kind of alluvial world, one that results from sand and silt accumulating where there should have been running water. Within it, there are four levels in which the player takes on the perspective of what are usually considered non-playable characters (NPCs). In doing so, Bucknell challenges traditional gameplay dynamics with what they refer to as ‘queer and ecological game mechanics.’

Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video game, 2024 Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video game, 2024

 

 

Exploring each level of The Alluvials

 

In one such level, Extinction Burst, the game opens with a bright red forest and a mountain lion sulking down a fuchsia path. The scene gives way to a dried-out basin filled with white particles. On closer inspection, these drifting flakes are revealed to be a form of moth. As you approach a desert tree growing near the center of the basin, a question in blue, hazy letters reveals itself in the sky: ‘ARE YOU MOTH OR ARE YOU TREE?’

 

Depending on whether you choose the moth or the tree path, you encounter two different perspectives of the desert landscape. In other levels, you walk through a forest fire, become a wolf in a barren, sand-ridden Los Angeles, or pilot an airboat speeding through the Los Angeles River in the evening. By presenting these alternative points of view, the game not only challenges the agency of the player but also offers an embodied understanding of things humans may take for granted: the knowledge of a moth, a yucca plant, or a body of water.

Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video game, 2024

Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video game, 2024

 

 

Exhibition history

 

The Alluvials has been exhibited in both film and video game formats in spaces across the US, Europe, and Asia. In each space, Bucknell’s work has come to life in different forms. When the film was shown at LUMINEX in downtown Los Angeles as a four-channel work, scenes of the forest fire lit up the building, creating an uncanny reflection of both the real and fictional city. At HEK Other Intelligences 2025 in Basel, Switzerland, pink camo beanbags were set up on the floor, presenting the game as a two-channel video game that visitors could sit down and play in a space awash in fuchsia.

Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video game, 2024

 

 

How to live in the world of The Alluvials

 

Within Bucknell’s work, the artist brings together a work of speculative fiction that speaks from an unknown future but carries the threatening tone of a parable lived many times over. True to the worlds they build in game engines, Bucknell makes real the promises, threats, and predictions that the CEOs and climate scientists of our time launch into the news cycle. Yet here, there is an opportunity to progress, level by level, through the very possible consequences of our present-day actions if left unchecked. Here, the posthuman utopia is one in which we leave humanity behind—one that discards the anthropocentric thinking that leads to artificial waters—and instead move forward with a future informed by the logic and processes of the natural world, before it is too late.

WOVEN BAMBOO pairs with CROCHETED TEXTILES IN CHAIR SCULPTURE

 

Designed by Peiyang (Camille) Li, She Blooms in the Dark is a glowing seating sculpture that examines emotional vulnerability and protective spatial thresholds. The project pairs hand-crocheted textile craft with traditional bent-wood furniture techniques, mounting an array of individual blossoms across the open-lattice framework of an oversized, woven bamboo armchair.


SHE BLOOMS IN THE DARK: Day Time View | all images ©Kexin Liu

 

 

UV-REACTIVE YARN BLOOMS STORE DAYLIGHT TO EMIT AMBIENT GLOW

 

The sculptural seat by designer Peiyang (Camille) Li functions simultaneously as a functional chair and a semi-enclosed personal retreat, utilizing a curved, basket-like silhouette to delineate an intimate interior microclimate for solitary reflection. Each floral element is crocheted by hand using phosphorescent, UV-reactive yarn engineered to absorb daylight and emit a soft, low-intensity glow in dark environments. By forgoing high-output electric fixtures in favor of photoluminescent fibers, the textile piece activates after dark, transforming the structural bamboo envelope into an illuminated shelter where soft woven media counterbalance the rigid natural framework.

we drove volvo’s electric EX60 to russel wright’s modernist woodland home at manitoga

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

an autumn drive into the hudson highlands

 

Volvo‘s new all-electric EX60 took us from Manhattan to Manitoga, the woodland home of American industrial designer Russel Wright in Garrison, New York. The drive north brought us out of the city and into the tree-lined roads of the Hudson Valley, glowing with autumn color, where the Swedish automaker had organized a design experience around its newest electric SUV.

 

During the mid-twentieth century, Wright spent decades transforming a former quarry into a 75-acre woodland estate. The experimental and iconic modernist home incorporates the surrounding forest into its architecture, with the resulting space feeling like an architect’s fairytale.

 

Volvo’s designers have been exploring similar ideas for the EX60, studying natural materials and the changing colors of the landscape to shape the experience inside the car.

images courtesy Volvo

 

 

the all-electric ex60

 

The EX60 translates the familiar proportions of Volvo’s popular XC60 into a fully electric SUV. Its low front end and gently sloping roofline contribute to a drag coefficient of just 0.26, while beneath the body sits the company’s new SPA3 electric architecture.

 

The platform introduces megacasting and cell-to-body battery integration to reduce weight and improve efficiency. For the U.S. market, the P10 AWD offers 503 horsepower and up to 330 miles of EPA-estimated range, with charging from 10 to 80 percent taking as little as 16 minutes under optimal conditions.

the EX60 builds on the familiar XC60 with a dedicated electric platform and more aerodynamic proportions

 

 

material and color choices

 

Inside, the EX60 introduces a 15-inch curved OLED center display with Google Gemini integrated into the infotainment system. The cabin has a fairly simple layout, with tactile finishes and natural textures softening the presence of its digital technology.

 

Once we arrived at Manitoga, we met Sara Erichsen Susnjar, Volvo Cars’ Senior Color & Material Design Manager, who has spent twenty years with the company and oversees color and material design for its midsize models.

 

Presenting a palette of materials, Susnjar drew our attention to the EX60’s available tailored wool-blend upholstery. Wool, she explained, naturally responds to body temperature, remaining comfortable through changes in weather. Its breathability is especially useful when returning to a parked car on a hot day. The material comes with challenges around production costs and customer expectations, although its practical qualities make a compelling case. ‘I think it’s a little bit brave, but also it has a function,‘ she told us.

the EX60’s interior pairs digital technology with natural textures and Scandinavian restraint

 

 

The conversation soon turned to color. Susnjar explained that developing a new automotive finish can involve an entire year of evaluation, with painted vehicles taken outdoors in different seasons to observe how their colors respond to changing light.

 

‘We have to check all the light,‘ she explained. This means moving between controlled lighting environments and outdoor settings, from warm summer sunlight to the colder light of winter.

the EX60 achieves a drag coefficient of 0.26, with efficiency influencing its exterior design

 

 

russel wright’s manitoga is a house shaped by the forest

 

There was an interesting historical parallel waiting inside Manitoga. Wright approached color as something that should respond to the seasons, changing elements of his home’s interior throughout the year. Warmer colors appeared during winter, while cooler tones were introduced for summer. Best known for his American Modern dinnerware, introduced in 1939, Wright had long been interested in how design could make domestic environments more comfortable and enjoyable.

 

In 1942, Wright and his wife Mary purchased the former quarry and logging property in Garrison. The estate would eventually become home to Dragon Rock, completed in 1961 with architect David Leavitt. Touring the house, we found interiors filled with unusual handmade experiments.

 

Large boulders extend into the rooms, while actual pine needles are embedded in green plaster walls. Wright even pressed butterfly wings or wildflowers between sheets of translucent plastic to creating decorative screens from materials gathered in nature.

exterior colors are tested across seasons to study their response to changing natural light

 

 

immersed in wild nature

 

Outside, a waterfall drops into the former quarry pool, while trails wind through the surrounding woodland. Our guide explained how Wright encouraged native moss to spread by removing competing plants to gradually transforming the forest floor.

 

Today, Manitoga is a National Historic Landmark, designated in 2006, and is maintained by the nonprofit Russel Wright Design Center, which continues to preserve and restore the house and landscape.

Sara Erichsen Susnjar discusses the EX60’s wool-blend upholstery and its temperature-regulating qualities

 

bringing the forest into the electric cabin

 

One of the EX60’s more unusual interior features is its Forest Light ambient lighting system, which projects a pattern of leaves onto the rear seats and center console. Susnjar described the feature during our visit, explaining that the design team wanted to bring some of the experience of nature into the vehicle. As the presentation took place during the day, we could only get a partial impression of the effect, which is designed to be experienced in darkness.

 

After exploring Dragon Rock, Wright experimented extensively with lighting. Large expanses of glass bring the changing woodland into view from almost every room. His home responds directly to its surroundings, with natural materials appearing throughout the architecture. Volvo takes a different approach, using contemporary technology to introduce an impression of the forest into the enclosed space of a car.

the EX60 introduces a new digital interface with a 15-inch curved OLED display

sunlight filtering through leaves inspired Volvo’s Forest Light ambient lighting system

Jeremiah Brent’s distinct approach to design is what drew rug and textile brand Loloi to partner with the interior designer on Rhythms of Craft, a recently launched collection of rugs and pillows. Jeremiah Brent × Loloi features more than 35 rugs with an architectural point of view and pillows that reference embroidered linens of the past.

Jeremiah Brent, pictured with hand-tufted wool rug Fori in Espresso Brown. Photography courtesy of Loloi.

Loloi principal Cyrus Loloi says his company has long admired Brent’s style, which Loloi describes as “grand and elegant” but with a “warmth and history”—a combination that felt like “a natural fit” for the brand. The brand also wanted to introduce something original to an oversaturated market, appreciating how Brent can reinterpret architecture, art, and history in a way that feels “fresh and relevant to how people live today.”

Fori rug in Rust Beige. Photography courtesy of Loloi

References to Mies van der Rohe, ancient Rome, and Gothic stonework prevail in Brent’s designs. “I was particularly interested in the language of architecture and how something structural can be equally impactful when it becomes soft and tactile,” he says. “I pulled from the lines, proportions, and repetition and let them become something new through the making.”

Alberti pillow in Natural Brown. Photography courtesy of Loloi.

A true creative partnership, the collection combines Brent’s perspective with Loloi’s expertise in rug design, materials, and construction. Sculptural silhouettes, carved details, and geometric patterns are realized through a range of techniques, including hand-tufted wool, hand-loomed blends, hand-woven jute, hand-knotted rugs, and power-loomed designs for high-traffic areas.

Tracery rug. Photography courtesy of Loloi.

Interestingly, both Brent and Loloi cite Obelisk as their favorite pattern of the line. “The notched corners, inspired by Art Deco architecture, give what might otherwise be a simple wool rug an entirely different character,” Loloi says.

“The Obelisk has made its way into more than one room [in our home],” Brent admits. “A notched edge has always been one of my favorite details to incorporate in stone, countertops, and door frames. So I love being able to bring that same detail onto the floor. It’s a simple addition but really impacts the tone of a room.”

Obelisk rug in Camel. Photography courtesy of Loloi

The Jeremiah Brent x Loloi collection is available at loloirugs.com and select online retailers.

ISERN SERRA CONCEALS RETRO COCKTAIL BAR BEHIND FOCACCERIA

 

Designed by Isern Serra for hospitality group Grupo La Confitería (led by Lito Baldovinos and Enric Rebordosa), Focacha is a speakeasy cocktail bar situated at Carrer Tuset 17 in Barcelona. The venue pays programmatic and aesthetic homage to the city’s 1960s and 1970s intellectual counterculture, the ‘gauche divine,’ and night-life figure Oriol Regàs, founder of the historic Bocaccio club. Concealed behind a traditional focacceria storefront designed by Pichy Glass, patrons access the venue through an operable refrigerator door, transitioning from a vernacular culinary retail interior into an immersive two-zone lounge modeled after Verner Panton’s 1968 Visiona installation.

all images ©Salva López

 

 

CHROMATIC bar interiors PAY HOMAGE TO VERNER PANTON’S VISIONA

 

The interior layout, developed by designer Isern Serra, divides into two distinct spatial volumes. The initial bar area is wrapped in continuous carpeting across the floor, walls, and ceiling, establishing a monomaterial envelope referencing Verner Panton’s 1968 Visiona installation. At the center of the room, a curved stainless-steel bar features rounded edge profiles that catch and redistribute ambient reflections. Surrounding the counter, seven distinct seating modules transition across a calibrated chromatic gradient from yellow to crimson and deep blue. Along the perimeter, curvilinear niches recessed into the upholstered walls accommodate both functional back-bar bottle shelving and secluded, low-slung banquette seating. Multi-Lite pendants by GUBI suspend directly overhead, illuminating the metallic work surfaces while highlighting the contrast between the reflective steel counter and the dense, sound-dampening textile surfaces.

Focacha is a hidden speakeasy cocktail bar designed by Isern Serra on Carrer Tuset in Barcelona

 

 

DOMED DJ LOUNGE INTEGRATES SCENOGRAPHIC LIGHTING design

 

The second spatial volume hosts the DJ room, creating a Retro-Futuristic setting. A circular portal cuts through the carpeted wall plane, transitioning into the adjoining music and DJ lounge. Centered under an organic vaulted dome, the volume explores Space Age aesthetics through fluid geometry and enveloping surfaces. Scenographic lighting systems are concealed within circular ceiling apertures and perimeter wall openings, allowing the light temperature and color spectrum to shift dynamically in response to musical tempo. Period-specific luminaires anchor the perimeter seating arrangements, including Louis Poulsen Panthella floor lamps, &Tradition Flowerpot VP2 pendants, and Wire Stool VP11 units, while a central Dana lamp by Tacchini acts as a sculptural focal point beneath the plaster canopy.

Cuberto Unveils Wickret UI/UX Design & Mobile Banking System

Perched above Seattle’s Alki Beach, Alki Lookout is a century-old home that has traded conventional coastal style for a palette of saturated colors, playful patterns, and warm wood. With sweeping views of Puget Sound, Bainbridge Island, and the Olympic Mountains, the home already had a spectacular setting. The interiors, however, lacked the same sense of character. Originally built as a log cabin in 1910 and remodeled twice over the years, the house lacked a cohesive identity. Seattle-based Shed saw an opportunity to transform the 2,706-square-foot residence into a playful retreat that reflects its surroundings without relying on familiar seaside motifs.

Photography by Rafael Soldi

“Our initial impressions of the home were that the home was perched above Alki Beach with a spectacular view of Elliott Bay and the Olympic Mountains but that it was fundamentally a home without a strong character,” says Prentis Hale, principal at Shed. Working within a budget that required retaining much of the existing structure, the team approached the renovation with targeted architectural changes and a more adventurous use of materials and color.

Photography by Rafael Soldi

The homeowners, who had both lived in Southern California, envisioned a home that captured the warmth, sunshine, and spontaneity of their shared beach experiences. Shed translated that vision into what the designers playfully describe as “a serious surf palette,” combining saturated colors, expressive patterns, and natural wood finishes.

Photography by Rafael Soldi

The transformation began with a deceptively simple move: swapping the kitchen and dining areas. Previously, the dining room occupied an awkward circulation zone, disconnected from the views that make the property so special. Now, a built-in dining banquette sits beside the window, where the family can gather and watch ferries cross Puget Sound.

Photography by Rafael Soldi

The reconfiguration also improved circulation, increased kitchen storage, and strengthened the connection between the cooking, dining, and living spaces. Custom vertical-grain fir cabinetry brings warmth to the kitchen, while pink Concrete Collaborative countertops and Zia Tile’s Pink Dahlia backsplash introduce a contrasting layer of color.

One of the home’s defining features is a tiled fin wall that helps organize the main living level. Clad in Alex Proba’s Moroccan Mix tile for Concrete Collaborative, its abstract shapes and soft salmon, yellow, and blue tones establish a palette that carries into the surrounding rooms.

Photography by Rafael Soldi

“The owners fell in love with the Alex Proba–designed ‘Moroccan Mix’ tile by Concrete Collaborative; the tiles then set the tone for the project,” says Hale. The team carried the colors throughout the house, establishing a sense of cohesion among the varied patterns and finishes.

In the living room, a pink sofa, custom fir screens, and built-in window seating create places for conversation and quiet moments overlooking the water. The warm wood surfaces and white oak flooring provide a quieter counterpoint to the home’s more expressive details.

Photography by Rafael Soldi

The bathrooms take a similarly playful approach. In the guest bathroom, undulating blue-and-white Zia Tile meets Hygge & West’s Underwater World wallpaper, bringing an abstract interpretation of the marine environment indoors. Elsewhere, geometric tile, terrazzo surfaces, and warm wood cabinetry extend the material palette into more private spaces.

Photography by Rafael Soldi

Upstairs, the primary suite demonstrates that practical improvements can be just as transformative as bold finishes. By reworking the stair area, Shed increased storage to approximately two and a half times its original capacity, incorporating closets, cabinetry, and a custom built-in bed with concealed drawers. A mirrored transom above the bedroom entry reflects light and creates a greater sense of height.

Photography by Rafael Soldi

The exterior’s most colorful addition emerged unexpectedly. When construction revealed that the siding needed replacement to resolve a waterproofing issue, the homeowners saw an opportunity to extend the interior’s personality outdoors. They commissioned Seattle artist Stevie Shao to create a vibrant mural celebrating local wildlife, including whales, osprey, and cranes, with an axolotl added at their daughter’s request.

Photography by Rafael Soldi

Visible from the beach below, the artwork transforms the home’s facade into a public-facing expression of the family’s personality. As Hale explains, the mural expresses the homeowners’ desire to share their creative spirit with the surrounding community.

At Alki Lookout, a relatively modest reconfiguration of the floor plan has an impact as significant as the bold material palette. Rather than simply framing the coastal landscape, Shed has found a way to bring its energy inside.

Experience Design: Dropping the “User” from “User Experience”

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Summary: AI is expanding UX beyond human users to agents acting on their behalf. Experience design names this broader field, which still centers on people’s goals.


I am not one for new terms. When NN/G changed its job titles from “user-experience specialist” to “experience specialist”, I was skeptical. The article we published to explain the change did not convince me either: yet another name for the same thing. I now think the new title was right, but for a reason we did not think of at the time. Our field is no longer only about people directly using digital products. It also includes agents that act on their behalf. UX is not disappearing. But AI is expanding the field in several directions at once.

UX Has Always Been More than UI

Don Norman helped popularize the term "user experience " in the 1990s because "usability" and "user interface" were too narrow. He wanted a name for everything a person experiences with a product, from the packaging to the support call. The term widened the object of design. Yet, the idea of a broader object of design did not really hit home until the 2010s, when modern smartphones rose to ubiquity, and people started using multiple channels to accomplish a goal. As a response, UX and design professionals began creating customer-journey maps and service blueprints to help them understand and track all the possible ways in which a user could interact with the organization while trying to achieve a goal. At that time, we began to understand that a good experience meant that all the touchpoints worked together coherently.

But throughout all these iterations of the work, one assumption stayed constant: that the human was the user — the one who interacts with the company directly, through one of many possible channels.

Summary: AI-native software development lifecycles reduce UX to AI context. UX must discover user needs, shape what’s built, and evaluate it before and after launch.

As a kid, Bram Vanderbeke wanted to be a bricklayer, so studying woodwork and construction made sense. But after studying design at the Royal Academy of Fine Arts in Ghent and graduating from Design Academy Eindhoven, he took his hands-on talents in a different direction, pursuing a career as an artist and designer in his hometown.

Vanderbeke explores the relationship between an object, its architectural environment, and its user, with a focus on raw materiality, form, and function. The result is tactile, sculptural work that often blurs the boundaries between art, design, and architecture.

“I just loved how architecture, art, and nature can merge so well with each other,” he says, citing Insel Hombroich, a museum and sculpture park in Neuss, Germany, as a lasting influence. Returning in different seasons, he was struck by how the changing landscape transformed the experience.

Bram Vanderbeke’s Anthology Side Table/Stool, designed in collaboration with Belgian jeweler Lore Van Keer, combines layers of cast concrete and aluminum. Photography by Amber Vanbossel.

Perhaps surprisingly, hardcore punk also informs his creative practice. “I grew up in this subculture, which is built on DIY shows and a very close community,” he says. Its DIY ethos and motivation to make things happen resonate with his own approach.

This fall, Vanderbeke presented Cavea, a group exhibition featuring more than 70 works by more than 40 designers, organized by Brut Collective, the Belgian collectible design group he cofounded in 2018, and DesignUnDisciplined. His recent work also includes designs for the newly reopened museum shop at Design Museum Gent and commissions for Bottega Veneta, Kelly Wearstler, Pieter Mulier and Lore Van Keer.

For today’s Friday Five, we spent five minutes with Bram Vanderbeke discussing the inspiration behind his collaboration with Lore Van Keer on the Anthology Side Table/Stool.

Photography by Amber Vanbossel

Anthology Side Table/Stool from the LVK x BV collection

Vanderbeke and Belgian jeweler Lore Van Keer bring together furniture and jewelry design in a collaboration that explores form, structure, and material: the LVK x BV collection of a silver necklace and sculptural stool, produced in limited editions of 20 and eight pieces, respectively.

The tactile stool features alternating layers of cast concrete and aluminum. Handcrafted in Vanderbeke’s Belgian atelier, each piece uses a new mold, making every one unique. Designed for indoor or outdoor use, the stool/table is made to order.

What Inspired LVK x BV Anthology Side Table/Stool?

Lore Van Keer’s Anthology 10 ring, with its stacked geometric forms, inspired Bram Vanderbeke’s sculptural side table/stool. Photography courtesy of Bram Vanderbeke.

Stacked Geometry

Lore Van Keer invited Vanderbeke to reinterpret her Anthology jewelry collection as a furniture piece. “I liked the stacked shapes of the collection,” he explains. “She also plays with circles and hexagon shapes, which I translated into the shapes of the Anthology Side Table.”

Specifically, the Anthology 10 ring inspired Vanderbeke to translate its rhythm of stacked forms and structures into a 3D object. Alternating concrete and aluminum create a layered composition that defines the form.

Waterfall for 666 Fifth Avenue, New York, just before its removal in 2020. Photography by Nicholas Knight © The Isamu Noguchi Foundation and Garden Museum, New York / Artists Rights Society (ARS), New York

Architectural Layers

For inspiration, Vanderbeke browsed a tome he keeps in his atelier: Isamu Noguchi’s monograph A Sculptor’s World. The book features the spectacular ceiling and waterfall Noguchi designed for the lobby of 666 Fifth Avenue in New York. Noting the ceiling’s layers of aluminum, Vanderbeke incorporated a similar approach into the Anthology piece.

Ceiling for 666 Fifth Avenue, New York, just before its removal in 2020. Photography by Nicholas Knight © The Isamu Noguchi Foundation and Garden Museum, New York / Artists Rights Society (ARS), New York

Ancient Forms

“I’m always inspired by excavations and ruins,” Vanderbeke says, “especially the rough stacked shapes of the Roman columns and their textures.” This influence connects directly to his Cast Bricks collection, which also informs the Anthology side table.

Ancient Roman columns and their stacked forms and textures are among the architectural influences behind Bram Vanderbeke’s sculptural designs. Photography courtesy of Vanderbeke.

Building Blocks

Vanderbeke also drew on his own work, incorporating elements from his Cast Bricks collection into the Anthology piece. For the Brick Chair, he arranged regular bricks in methodical yet intuitive compositions that create negative spaces, which serve as molds for his architectural furniture. The straightforward technique references how construction workers lay the foundations of a building, connecting back to Vanderbeke’s childhood fascination with bricklaying.

a new home for AC milan and inter is taking shape

 

Foster + Partners and MANICA have unveiled the design for Generali Stadium in Milan, a new 71,500-seat football arena that will become the shared home of AC Milan and FC Internazionale Milano. Planned for the San Siro district, the stadium will feature an exposed concrete X-frame structure and a sweeping double-curved roof. Its design draws from the city’s architectural heritage, with a repeating geometric facade that references the intricate stonework of Milan’s Duomo.

 

The proposal arrives after years of debate over the future of the existing San Siro, officially known as Stadio Giuseppe Meazza, which has hosted Milan’s football clubs for generations. The new stadium will rise beside the historic venue, with plans calling for the partial demolition of the older structure following the move. Foster + Partners and MANICA have approached the project with attention to the atmosphere of San Siro, whose steep seating and proximity to the field have long shaped the experience of attending a match.

visualizations © Foster + Partners, MANICA

 

 

concrete X-frames shape the stadium’s sculptural facade

 

Generali Stadium’s exterior is defined by a series of intersecting concrete supports that extend from the ground-level colonnades to the roof. Foster and Partners and MANICA further use these X-shaped elements to establish a strong geometric rhythm around the oval building. At the entrances, diamond-shaped openings offer glimpses into the stadium, leading visitors toward naturally ventilated circulation spaces and panoramic concourses overlooking the city.

 

Above the seating bowl, a double-curved roof follows the changing height of the stands, rising around the two ends of the stadium. Its spoke-wheel structure covers the spectator seating while leaving the central playing area open to the sky. The roof will incorporate approximately 27,500 square meters of photovoltaic panels, with acoustic design intended to contain and amplify the sound of supporters inside the arena.

Foster + Partners and MANICA unveil their design for the 71,500-seat Generali Stadium in Milan

 

 

steep seating walls bring supporters closer to the pitch

 

Inside, the architects have organized the stadium around a continuous 360-degree seating bowl divided into two tiers. The layout is designed to maintain clear sightlines across the 71,500 seats and draw spectators closer to the field. At opposite ends, two large supporter sections, known in Italian football as the Curvas, rise steeply behind the goals. Each will host about 11,700 fans, with seating angles reaching 37 degrees.

 

These two walls of seating form some of the most distinctive spaces within the design. Their steep inclines concentrate spectators directly behind the goals, maintaining the intensity associated with San Siro’s existing stands. The seating configuration also allows the arena to retain an intimate relationship between supporters and players despite its colossal scale. Acoustic considerations extend into the roof geometry, which has been shaped to help direct crowd noise back toward the field.

a repeating concrete X-frame structure extends across the stadium’s facade

 

 

generali stadium forms part of a wider urban redevelopment

 

Beyond the stadium itself, the proposal forms part of a 281,000-square-meter redevelopment of the San Siro district. New public spaces and landscaped areas will surround the venue, with leisure and retail facilities intended to attract visitors throughout the year. The stadium is also planned to accommodate concerts and other major events to extend its use beyond the football calendar.

 

The development incorporates an all-electric operational strategy, natural ventilation, and systems for rainwater harvesting and greywater recycling. The architects are targeting LEED Gold certification and carbon-neutral operations.

 

Following the design unveiling on October 29th, 2026, the project will advance through further planning and approval stages. Construction is currently expected to begin in 2028, with the first match targeted for the 2031/32 season.

Summer lighting comes easy. Just open the blinds and let the sunshine in. But as the days grow shorter and the nights grow longer, we turn indoors for cozier ways to light a space. Think of the warmth of a glowing window lit from inside, or a snug reading nook warmed by soft lighting. As the seasons shift into autumn, natural daylight gives way to artificial lighting, which becomes the primary way to restore warmth to the home.

Still, the darker months offer a great opportunity to fundamentally alter a space’s essence without buying new furniture, painting the walls, or making permanent changes. That’s where lighting comes in. With the ability to transform a home’s emotional temperature—from cool whites to a warm, sunset-like glow—lighting is key to adapting a home to the changing seasons.

“Layering warm light fills the visual void left by early dusk, softening cold architectural surfaces and mimicking the natural amber warmth of sunset,” says Sherry Ye, chief marketing officer at global smart-lighting brand Govee. “It allows us to reclaim our living spaces as comforting sanctuaries during the darker months.”

Design Each Room Around How You Want It to Feel

Govee’s layered lighting guide identifies three key zones—the living room, bedroom, and home office—and flexible smart lighting solutions to shape mood and adapt each space for the changing seasons.

Photography courtesy of Govee

Cozy Connection in the Living Room

Lighting has the power to transform a space, but it is equally impactful in how it makes you feel. From placement to color, different combinations create different emotional effects. In the living room, the goal is to create a cocoon-like sanctuary that feels warm and comfortable while encouraging socializing and connection. The strategy: layered lighting at varying heights.

Surround the seating area with a cozy glow using the Govee Floor Lamp 3, a double-sided accent lamp that will wash a corner and wall in soft light. Add the Table Lamp on a side table to create another level, then place the Govee TV Backlight 3 behind the screen to soften the contrast between a bright display and a dark wall. Together they bathe the space in rich golden light that doesn’t overpower the room but draws people inward for an intimate—and inviting—atmosphere. Plus, all three can be dimmed and adjusted together from one app, so the scene can soften gradually as evening settles in.

Photography courtesy of Govee

Peace of Mind in the Bedroom

The bedroom calls for a different lighting approach. Instead of focusing on sanctuary and connection, this space prioritizes rest and relaxation. The result is soft, indirect lighting that naturally aligns with the day’s cycles as the body winds down into sleep.

For a low-intensity scheme that maintains a restful atmosphere, try layering the Govee Lantern Floor Lamp and Govee Table Lamp Classic with the soft halo effect of the Govee COB Strip Light 2 Pro. When applied to headboards or other surfaces, the strip offers a subtle glow in desaturated blue and purple accents, plus warm 2500K–2700K light. With the floor lamp’s wide reach and the table lamp’s gentle wash, this combination helps calm the mind with balanced, restful lighting before sleep.

Photography courtesy of Govee

Friendly Focus in the Home Office

In the home office, functionality is key. Yet, there is a fine line between a streamlined design and a space that feels sterile. Here, the strategy is balancing clear, functional task lighting with soft, accent lighting.

Anchor the room with the Govee Sky Ceiling Light, whose clear, energizing white light in the 4000K–6000K range brings concentration and visibility. Then soften the edges with the Govee Tree Floor Lamp and Govee Table Lamp Classic as soft, warm accents that keep the room from feeling clinical. “This contrast prevents eye fatigue, keeps the mind alert, and ensures the room feels human and inviting, even during long winter work sessions,” explains Ye.

Shaping Mood Through Color and Placement

Brightness may be one of the defining tools of the lighting toolbox, but color temperature, effects, and placement also significantly shape a room’s mood.

Working both on a physiological and psychological level, the body registers the difference between a cozy reading nook and cool task lighting in the kitchen immediately. In the first, a warm glow invites intimacy and comfort; in the second, crisp white light sharpens focus. “Color temperature sets our biological clock,” Ye says. “Warm, low-Kelvin light (candlelight to warm white) signals safety and rest, while cool white light promotes alertness and focus.”

Visual effects like gradients also tap into the body’s response, mirroring nature’s gradual tonal shifts. Plus, playing with saturation and gradients introduces depth and texture to otherwise static or flat walls. Govee’s advanced color-blending technology is built for exactly this, offering single fixture or light strips with smooth, multi-tone color transitions that bring out the character of natural materials like wood grain, linen, and stone.

Placement is also key. Strategically placed accent lighting can spotlight hanging artwork, plants, backsplashes, or other design moments, while indirect lighting can soften a hard surface or bring out color and depth. For homeowners who want to experiment without the expensive rewiring, flexible smart lighting solutions like Govee’s make room to test placement, along with brightness, color, and atmosphere, until the desired mood takes shape.

Photography courtesy of Govee

Making Seasonal Changes Without Redesigning the Room

Homeowners instinctively understand the power of lighting. It underscores every impactful design moment in a home. “Light is the invisible architecture of a space,” affirms Ye. “You can furnish a room with the finest materials and art, but without considered lighting, those elements remain flat and uninspired. Light dictates how we perceive color, texture, scale, and proportion.”

As the seasons turn and life moves indoors for the colder months, that influence matters more than ever, and resonant lighting becomes essential.

Lighting, fortunately, adapts with the seasons, reenergizing a home without new furniture or a swap of old décor. In fall and winter, that means cozy corners and soft, intimate interiors. Govee’s portfolio of flexible smart lighting makes the shift effortless. Plus, when the holidays arrive, strategic decorative lighting with color-changing capabilities can level up the festive feel while maintaining visual cohesion.

THISS STUDIO PUTS RADICAL REUSE BEFORE THE FINISHED DRAWING

 

An old barn, a pontoon boat, and a dismantled exhibition might seem like unlikely starting points for an architectural brief. For THISS Studio, they offer materials with which to begin designing. Founded by architects Tamsin Hanke and Sash Scott, the London practice works across cultural spaces, fashion, retail, and public installations, developing projects around the people and resources available to make them. Hanke’s research into low-impact design meets Scott’s interest in material experimentation and structural invention, an approach informed in part by his experience designing and flying high-performance kites. In an exclusive interview with designboom, Hanke explains how working with reclaimed components changes the architect’s role, the construction process, and the expectations attached to a finished space.

 

‘Primarily, reuse changes the order in which we design,’ Hanke tells designboom. A project can no longer be fully resolved on paper before its materials have been found. Instead, the studio establishes its intentions, gathers available stock, and revisits the design as that stock arrives, continuing to make adjustments during construction. Suppliers, craftspeople, and the dimensions and condition of individual components all influence the outcome. ‘With reuse, necessity means we can no longer be the sole authors of the outcome,’ he explains. This requires a strong concept and details capable of accommodating differences between materials. As Hanke puts it, ‘The work becomes a conversation rather than a monologue.’

Tamsin Hanke and Sash Scott, founders of THISS Studio | image by Jasper Fry

 

 

SALVAGED TIMBER AND AN UPTURNED BOAT SHAPE NEW SPACES

 

That willingness to let existing objects redirect a project is visible in Barn Again, developed with architect Tom Svilans for the 2025 Copenhagen Architecture Biennial. Beams recovered from disused Norwegian barns are stacked into a tapering pavilion, retaining weathered surfaces and traces of previous connections. Designed for disassembly and further use, the structure gives those irregularities a place in its architectural expression. At Kiko Kostadinov’s London flagship, a pontoon boat installation by artist Ryan Trecartin provides a very different starting point: inverted railings become a ceiling-mounted support system for the shop’s shelving. ‘Often the material will suggest a move we would never have drawn from scratch,’ Hanke says. Both projects make that possibility tangible, finding a new spatial role for something with an established identity.

 

The same attention to available resources extends to Pergola, a public outdoor theater in Cairo developed with CLUSTER and Orient Productions. Its red structure incorporates materials from local reuse initiatives, including textiles made from recovered river plastic by VeryNile and recycled-plastic bricks from Rebloxx. Elsewhere, the studio’s work on PoliNations demonstrates how a temporary installation can continue beyond its original setting. First presented in Birmingham in 2022, its monumental tree structures returned at Glastonbury’s Shangri-La in 2025. These projects approach material life at different scales, from individual components to substantial structures that can be used again. ‘Reuse should never dilute the conceptual ambition of a project,’ Hanke argues. ‘We are happily now in a position in the industry where reuse is not even enough to drive the design, it is just part of the process.’

monumental tree structures frame a temporary garden in Birmingham

 

 

EXHIBITION DESIGN STARTS WITH WHAT HAPPENS AFTERWARDS

 

For THISS Studio, exhibition design brings these questions into particularly sharp focus. A display may occupy a gallery briefly, yet its construction can determine whether its materials remain useful long after visitors leave. ‘Exhibition design is never neutral,’ Hanke says. ‘It participates in the conversation the exhibition is having with the work, and that conversation is ideological, even political.’ Discussing work with the V&A, he describes an all-timber assembly without metal fixings, conceived to preserve the components for subsequent use. The design considers how pieces will come apart and what condition they will be in when they do. ‘As we see it, an exhibition should be an interruption in a material’s life not the end of it.’

 

Working with the Design Museum has prompted another shift in method: taking stock before drawing. The studio catalogued and assessed existing exhibition infrastructure, then tracked design decisions against that inventory, scrutinizing each proposed addition. ‘We worked more like archaeologists or archivists than conventional architects,’ Hanke explains. Here, the challenge becomes how to alter the experience of a space through careful adjustments to what is already available. ‘The creative challenge is restraint: making something feel new while touching as little as possible.’ The approach gives the less visible work of sorting, documenting, and evaluating materials a direct influence on the architecture visitors eventually encounter.

repurposed boat railings support shelving inside Kiko Kostadinov’s store

 

 

MAKING REUSE WORK MEANS CHANGING THE COMMISSION

 

Turning this approach into routine practice requires institutions to reconsider how they commission and fund design. Hanke identifies a tension between the certainty clients expect at the outset and the adjustments that reclaimed materials can demand later. Design programs need to accommodate those revisions, while funding must arrive early enough to secure stock before rapid exhibition construction schedules take over. He points to organizations such as Yes Make, which can source and hold materials while a design develops. ‘Money and materials need to move earlier,’ he says. A willingness to reuse therefore has practical consequences for budgets, procurement, and decision-making, well before the first component reaches the gallery.

 

Long-term galleries introduce further pressures, particularly around maintenance and durability. Hanke argues that these requirements need not exclude previously used materials, citing the studio’s research into the recovery of hardwoods and cross-laminated timber after extended service. What matters is designing both the assembly and its eventual separation with sufficient care. ‘Dismantling should be designed, not improvised. The materials should leave as a resource not as waste.’ For THISS Studio, the architect’s responsibility consequently extends beyond the appearance and performance of an installation during its public life. It includes organizing the collaboration that produces it and anticipating what its parts might become once the exhibition is over.
