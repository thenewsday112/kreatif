Jeremiah Brent’s distinct approach to design is what drew rug and textile brand Loloi to partner with the interior designer on Rhythms of Craft, a recently launched collection of rugs and pillows. Jeremiah Brent × Loloi features more than 35 rugs with an architectural point of view and pillows that reference embroidered linens of the past.

Jeremiah Brent, pictured with hand-tufted wool rug Fori in Espresso Brown. Photography courtesy of Loloi.

Loloi principal Cyrus Loloi says his company has long admired Brent’s style, which Loloi describes as “grand and elegant” but with a “warmth and history”—a combination that felt like “a natural fit” for the brand. The brand also wanted to introduce something original to an oversaturated market, appreciating how Brent can reinterpret architecture, art, and history in a way that feels “fresh and relevant to how people live today.”

Fori rug in Rust Beige. Photography courtesy of Loloi

References to Mies van der Rohe, ancient Rome, and Gothic stonework prevail in Brent’s designs. “I was particularly interested in the language of architecture and how something structural can be equally impactful when it becomes soft and tactile,” he says. “I pulled from the lines, proportions, and repetition and let them become something new through the making.”

Alberti pillow in Natural Brown. Photography courtesy of Loloi.

A true creative partnership, the collection combines Brent’s perspective with Loloi’s expertise in rug design, materials, and construction. Sculptural silhouettes, carved details, and geometric patterns are realized through a range of techniques, including hand-tufted wool, hand-loomed blends, hand-woven jute, hand-knotted rugs, and power-loomed designs for high-traffic areas.

Tracery rug. Photography courtesy of Loloi.

Interestingly, both Brent and Loloi cite Obelisk as their favorite pattern of the line. “The notched corners, inspired by Art Deco architecture, give what might otherwise be a simple wool rug an entirely different character,” Loloi says.

“The Obelisk has made its way into more than one room [in our home],” Brent admits. “A notched edge has always been one of my favorite details to incorporate in stone, countertops, and door frames. So I love being able to bring that same detail onto the floor. It’s a simple addition but really impacts the tone of a room.”

Obelisk rug in Camel. Photography courtesy of Loloi

THISS STUDIO PUTS RADICAL REUSE BEFORE THE FINISHED DRAWING

 

An old barn, a pontoon boat, and a dismantled exhibition might seem like unlikely starting points for an architectural brief. For THISS Studio, they offer materials with which to begin designing. Founded by architects Tamsin Hanke and Sash Scott, the London practice works across cultural spaces, fashion, retail, and public installations, developing projects around the people and resources available to make them. Hanke’s research into low-impact design meets Scott’s interest in material experimentation and structural invention, an approach informed in part by his experience designing and flying high-performance kites. In an exclusive interview with designboom, Hanke explains how working with reclaimed components changes the architect’s role, the construction process, and the expectations attached to a finished space.

 

‘Primarily, reuse changes the order in which we design,’ Hanke tells designboom. A project can no longer be fully resolved on paper before its materials have been found. Instead, the studio establishes its intentions, gathers available stock, and revisits the design as that stock arrives, continuing to make adjustments during construction. Suppliers, craftspeople, and the dimensions and condition of individual components all influence the outcome. ‘With reuse, necessity means we can no longer be the sole authors of the outcome,’ he explains. This requires a strong concept and details capable of accommodating differences between materials. As Hanke puts it, ‘The work becomes a conversation rather than a monologue.’

Tamsin Hanke and Sash Scott, founders of THISS Studio | image by Jasper Fry

 

 

SALVAGED TIMBER AND AN UPTURNED BOAT SHAPE NEW SPACES

 

That willingness to let existing objects redirect a project is visible in Barn Again, developed with architect Tom Svilans for the 2025 Copenhagen Architecture Biennial. Beams recovered from disused Norwegian barns are stacked into a tapering pavilion, retaining weathered surfaces and traces of previous connections. Designed for disassembly and further use, the structure gives those irregularities a place in its architectural expression. At Kiko Kostadinov’s London flagship, a pontoon boat installation by artist Ryan Trecartin provides a very different starting point: inverted railings become a ceiling-mounted support system for the shop’s shelving. ‘Often the material will suggest a move we would never have drawn from scratch,’ Hanke says. Both projects make that possibility tangible, finding a new spatial role for something with an established identity.

 

The same attention to available resources extends to Pergola, a public outdoor theater in Cairo developed with CLUSTER and Orient Productions. Its red structure incorporates materials from local reuse initiatives, including textiles made from recovered river plastic by VeryNile and recycled-plastic bricks from Rebloxx. Elsewhere, the studio’s work on PoliNations demonstrates how a temporary installation can continue beyond its original setting. First presented in Birmingham in 2022, its monumental tree structures returned at Glastonbury’s Shangri-La in 2025. These projects approach material life at different scales, from individual components to substantial structures that can be used again. ‘Reuse should never dilute the conceptual ambition of a project,’ Hanke argues. ‘We are happily now in a position in the industry where reuse is not even enough to drive the design, it is just part of the process.’

monumental tree structures frame a temporary garden in Birmingham

 

 

EXHIBITION DESIGN STARTS WITH WHAT HAPPENS AFTERWARDS

 

For THISS Studio, exhibition design brings these questions into particularly sharp focus. A display may occupy a gallery briefly, yet its construction can determine whether its materials remain useful long after visitors leave. ‘Exhibition design is never neutral,’ Hanke says. ‘It participates in the conversation the exhibition is having with the work, and that conversation is ideological, even political.’ Discussing work with the V&A, he describes an all-timber assembly without metal fixings, conceived to preserve the components for subsequent use. The design considers how pieces will come apart and what condition they will be in when they do. ‘As we see it, an exhibition should be an interruption in a material’s life not the end of it.’

 

Working with the Design Museum has prompted another shift in method: taking stock before drawing. The studio catalogued and assessed existing exhibition infrastructure, then tracked design decisions against that inventory, scrutinizing each proposed addition. ‘We worked more like archaeologists or archivists than conventional architects,’ Hanke explains. Here, the challenge becomes how to alter the experience of a space through careful adjustments to what is already available. ‘The creative challenge is restraint: making something feel new while touching as little as possible.’ The approach gives the less visible work of sorting, documenting, and evaluating materials a direct influence on the architecture visitors eventually encounter.

repurposed boat railings support shelving inside Kiko Kostadinov’s store

 

 

MAKING REUSE WORK MEANS CHANGING THE COMMISSION

 

Turning this approach into routine practice requires institutions to reconsider how they commission and fund design. Hanke identifies a tension between the certainty clients expect at the outset and the adjustments that reclaimed materials can demand later. Design programs need to accommodate those revisions, while funding must arrive early enough to secure stock before rapid exhibition construction schedules take over. He points to organizations such as Yes Make, which can source and hold materials while a design develops. ‘Money and materials need to move earlier,’ he says. A willingness to reuse therefore has practical consequences for budgets, procurement, and decision-making, well before the first component reaches the gallery.

 

Long-term galleries introduce further pressures, particularly around maintenance and durability. Hanke argues that these requirements need not exclude previously used materials, citing the studio’s research into the recovery of hardwoods and cross-laminated timber after extended service. What matters is designing both the assembly and its eventual separation with sufficient care. ‘Dismantling should be designed, not improvised. The materials should leave as a resource not as waste.’ For THISS Studio, the architect’s responsibility consequently extends beyond the appearance and performance of an installation during its public life. It includes organizing the collaboration that produces it and anticipating what its parts might become once the exhibition is over.

HERZOG & DE MEURON REVEALS EAMES INSTITUTE MUSEUM PLANS

 

A warehouse with a roof this expressive already has something to exhibit. In Novato, California, Herzog & de Meuron is reworking the former Birkenstock campus into the future museum of the Eames Institute of Infinite Curiosity, preserving its sculptural concrete silhouette while changing what happens beneath it. Newly released renderings show the proposal taking shape with San Francisco practice EHDD as executive architect and Field Operations designing the landscape. Across an approximately 88-acre site, the project brings exhibition spaces, workshops, a theater, and gardens into a cultural destination whose ambitions extend beyond the work of Charles and Ray Eames.

 

The existing campus was designed for publisher McGraw-Hill by Jan Lubicz-Nycz for the office of John Savage Bolles, before becoming associated with Birkenstock. Its most recognizable element is a roof assembled from repeated hyperbolic paraboloid shells, developed with structural engineer Raj Desai. These curved surfaces use geometry to span large distances with remarkably little material, producing a sequence of pointed edges that gives the industrial building its unexpected drama. Retaining this structure makes the museum itself an example of design ingenuity: visitors will encounter an experiment in construction before reaching the first exhibition.

the museum is targeting an opening in 2030 | renders by Herzog & De Meuron

 

 

A REFLECTIVE BRIDGE REFRAMES THE CONCRETE WAREHOUSE

 

Herzog & de Meuron organizes the interior around a generous central hall, keeping the roof’s geometry visible overhead. An entrance court cut into an existing earth berm establishes the approach, while a steel-clad bridge introduces an elevated route through the building. Its reflective finish will catch fragments of the surrounding space, setting a visually shifting surface against the weight of the concrete canopy. Below, circulation routes connect exhibitions with places to gather and work. Building services and lighting will be integrated into the ceiling to reduce the visual clutter beneath its peaks.

 

The museum’s program gives visitors opportunities to see how things are made alongside the finished works. Studios and workshops for wood and ceramics sit within an arrangement of flexible exhibition spaces, allowing creative activity to become part of the visit. This approach also informs the broader collecting strategy, which encompasses industrial design, painting, sculpture, and other disciplines. The Eames legacy provides a point of departure for a museum interested in experimentation across generations, with space for the practical, occasionally messy business that precedes an object’s arrival on a pedestal.

the former Birkenstock campus will become the Eames Institute Museum

 

 

FIELD OPERATIONS BRINGS THE EAMES MUSEUM INTO THE LANDSCAPE

 

At the building’s northern and southern edges, recessed glass façades create deep covered areas between the interiors and gardens. These sheltered thresholds extend the possibilities for exhibitions and everyday use, while former loading zones become places to spend time outdoors. Field Operations connects ten gardens through paths, introducing native species including oak, manzanita, and California poppy. Planting draws on the region’s different habitats, from woodland to coastal prairie, giving the grounds a character rooted in Marin County rather than treating them as a uniform backdrop.

 

Outdoor sculpture, places to play, and areas for communal events will share this landscape with quieter spaces for sitting and walking. The proposal allows a museum visit to unfold across different settings and at different speeds, from a workshop or performance to an afternoon outside. Following the Institute’s acquisition of the property in 2025, the masterplan has been submitted to the City of Novato. Major construction is intended to begin in 2028, with 2030 set as the target opening year.

Scandinavian design is celebrated for light-filled spaces, pale woods, clean lines, and a sense of calm. Translating that aesthetic to a cruise ship, however, presents a spatial challenge—particularly when compared with the backdrop of the oversized, ornate interiors typical of mass-market cruise liners.

Sunlight pours through the lounge’s expansive windows, illuminating pale wood, textured upholsterery, and warm leather furnishings that lend the space a residential feel. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Viking has made Scandinavian modernism central to its fleet’s design identity, creating onboard spaces that feel less like conventional cruise ships and more like considered residential interiors. Across the vessels, the design language reflects a contemporary interpretation of Scandinavian aesthetics, sometimes called “New Nordic,” through natural textures, tactile materials, subtle color, and sculptural, carefully proportioned furnishings.

A sculptural wood lattice ceiling beneath expansive glazing brings natural light and organic forms into the Wintergarden lounge. Photography courtesy of Viking.

“Although ‘new Nordic’ is not terminology we use formally, traditional Scandinavian principles have evolved and are being interpreted for how people live and travel today,” says Richard Riveire, founding principal of Rottet Studio and the designer of Viking’s river, ocean, and expedition ships. “There’s still a strong sense of restraint, but it’s balanced by a feeling of being lived in. These days, Scandinavian design is less about minimalism and more about creating spaces that feel welcoming, thoughtful, and enduring.”

A deep blue built-in bookcase displays books, maps, shells, coral, and collected objects, connecting the residential atmosphere of Viking’s interiors with the theme of exploration. Photography courtesy of Viking

Drawing On Nordic Principles

Instead of treating Scandinavian design as a static visual style, Riveire and his team anchored the fleet’s interiors around five underlying principles: residential modernism, nature, craft, heritage, and exploration. This framework establishes a restrained architectural foundation, while fabrics, upholstery, rugs, and furniture introduce warmth and texture. “These tactile details make each space feel inviting and personal, while preserving the simplicity that is so central to Scandinavian design,” says Riveire.

An artwork depicting a figure on a beach anchors the atrium stairwell, bringing Nordic art into one of the ship’s main circulation spaces. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Tapping Into Nordic Heritage

Throughout Viking’s fleet, Nordic heritage appears in a collection of regional artwork, including pieces by celebrated Norwegian painter Edvard Munch. Textiles and decorative objects extend those cultural references, featuring fabrics by Dutch designer Hella Jongerius alongside tabletop accessories by Finnish architect Alvar Aalto.

“That heritage connects the design to Norwegian culture and folklore, while exploration reminds us that this is a ship designed for people who are curious about exploring the world,” says Riveire. “So maps, globes, libraries, constellations and other references to exploration play a meaningful role in our designs.”

A traditional Norwegian Marius blanket brings a familiar geometric knitting pattern into the staterooms, referencing a longstanding Norwegian textile tradition. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Mastering Materiality and Craft

Material choices help give Viking’s expansive public spaces a more intimate, residential character. Light woods, natural woven textiles, and stone bring warmth, texture, and visual depth to the staterooms and public areas. “Craftsmanship is fundamental because it gives a space a sense of humanity and authenticity,” says Riveire. “Our design calls for furniture, artwork, textiles, and decor that evoke handmade traditions or honor historic craftwork.” These references to craft appear throughout the ship, in details such as woven leather lounge chairs in the living room areas, the intricate ceiling latticework, and sculptural wood wall accents.

Dramatic glass walls surround the spa’s hydrotherapy pool, pairing natural stone, sculptural driftwood, and wood loungers to create a serene retreat that brings Scandinavian simplicity indoors. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Integrating Biophilic Design

The Nordic landscape—where birch trees, flora, and wildlife symbolize renewal—informs both the materials and sensory details throughout the ships. References to driftwood, lichen, and moss can be found in staterooms and lounges. In the public bathrooms, guests hear birds softly chirping in the background, while birch tree branches in planters and floor-to-ceiling forest wallpaper appear throughout the common areas and spa. “It’s where the landscape and wildlife outside become part of the indoor experience,” says Riveire.

In the spa, amenities such as a snow grotto, cold plunge, and thalassotherapy pool draw on Nordic bathing traditions. “Water reinforces the connection to the outdoors, making the spa an extension of the Nordic design philosophy,” says Riveire. Slate, stone, and glass reinforce the spa’s restrained, minimalist material palette.

Navy and slate blue stripes add a measured dose of color to the upholstered headboard, complementing the stateroom’s otherwise understated palette. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Layering Subtle Color

Rather than relying on stark whites or an entirely neutral palette, the ship’s interiors layer warm creams, sandy tans, and other earthy tones drawn from nature. Navy and slate blue stripes appear on upholstered stateroom headboards, while translucent aqua glass side plates and water glasses introduce light-reflecting accents to restaurant table settings. Orange and rust accents in throw pillows and carpets provide contrast without disrupting the overall palette. “These touches reflect a more expressive side of Scandinavian design,” says Riveire. “We want guests to experience color, but without introducing a lot of visual noise.”

A wall of glass frames ocean views from the pool deck, where pale loungers, blue-and-white textiles, and natural wood maintain the understated palette. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Framing the Natural Landscape

Floor-to-ceiling windows do more than admit daylight; their placement turns the passing landscape into a focal point of the interiors. “A Norwegian fjord, European river, or Antarctic landscape becomes part of the room, an ever-changing piece of art within the interior,” Riveire says. Sightlines and spatial planning help connect the rooms to their changing surroundings.

On land, designers can anticipate the position of the sun and how daylight will move through a home or building. “At sea, the sun is far more variable—not only in direction, but also in duration,” Riveire says. “During Arctic summers, for example, it may never set.” The interiors must therefore accommodate changing light conditions throughout a voyage. Architectural openings, curtains, and shades adjust as conditions shift, balancing dynamic daylight with interior comfort and privacy. “Spaces are connected to the world outside, while also creating more intimate moments indoors,” says Riveire.

A large window frames the passing river landscape, while pale wood, soft neutrals, and streamlined furnishings keep the suite’s interior visually quiet. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Concealing Technology and Softening Acoustics

A residential atmosphere depends on more than visual details. Sound, temperature, and lighting also shape how comfortable a space feels. To the interiors’ uncluttered appearance, technology is integrated discreetly into wood joinery and architectural details, such as underfloor heating in stateroom bathrooms or subtle lighting controls. Acoustic design plays an equally important role in creating a sense of hygge at sea; sound-absorbing textile wall panels, acoustic carpet underlays, and upholstered surfaces help reduce ambient noise in public areas. “In the Scandinavian tradition, design serves human comfort without demanding attention,” says Riveire. “Technology and acoustics are integrated so subtly that you don’t notice how the space functions—only how calm it makes you feel.”

Maritime artifacts, books, a globe, and warm wood shelving create a residential-style display that reflects Viking’s Scandinavian emphasis on craftsmanship and history. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Designing Intimate Spaces at Scale

Capturing a lived-in, residential warmth can be challenging on a vessel with expansive public areas like multi-level atriums, especially when Scandinavian design favors intimate and approachable spaces. “Creating a residential feeling within a ship requires careful attention to proportion,” says Riveire. “At Viking, we subscribe to a ‘cocoon’ principle—a house-within-a-house concept—where the interior provides a quiet and serene onboard environment, rather than constantly competing for attention.” This principle guides the arrangement of open, social hubs and smaller, quiet seating nooks. “Every furniture grouping has been carefully considered for how guests actually move through and use the space,” adds Riveire.

Henry Chalfant's legendary New York subway graffiti photos pull into Amsterdam at life size

Amsterdam's Straat offers a masterclass in the tricky balancing act of bringing graffiti out of the streets and into the museum: its new show of Henry Chalfant's sublime documentation of New York's nascent 1980s scene does it beautifully.

The art world, and society far more broadly, has had a tempestuous relationship with "street art" over the decades. It's been met with pretty much every response imaginable: in the early days, publicly denigrated by hand-wringing Mary Whitehouse types as a scourge on society, a symbol of ASBO youth writ large on walls, trains and the backs of bus seats.

Later on, street art – in certain specific pockets of the world at least – was firmly cemented into the commercial art world; a signifier of a certain pre-2008-crash, Shoreditchy hipsterness, when it was par for the course for street art to be shown in galleries (some with more longevity than others).

Not long after those wildly carefree, neon-and-powder-sprinkled years, though, street art seemed to lose its hip sheen. A friend of mine has long quipped that her number one red flag in a man is if she "goes back to his place and finds the Banksy coffee table book proudly on show". An arguably irrational ick, but I get it: I'd be straight out the door if a potential amour expressed a love of the Red Hot Chili Peppers, or, even worse, called them "the Chillis" (shudder). But I digress.

All of this is, of course, a highly truncated, reductive and rather subjective overview of a vastly expansive movement, one that deserves much more. And while trends are fleeting, something that remains a constant is how difficult it is to show art born in and of the streets in a gallery or museum. Safe to say, Straat in Amsterdam has absolutely nailed it.

A familiar post-industrial trajectory

The vast Straat Museum building – formerly the site where huge ships were built – sits on the NDSM (Nederlandsche Dok en Scheepsbouw Maatschappij, a former Dutch shipbuilding company) wharf in Amsterdam Noord, a short ferry ride across the water from Centraal Station.

The shipyard closed in 1984, and the site lay derelict; then a trajectory familiar to most cities began to unfold: buildings were squatted and then colonised by artists, and as such, things were beginning to be vaguely patched up into live-work-ish spaces throughout the 90s. Today, those huge, arguably brutishly industrially ugly warehouse buildings have evolved into legit art studios, event spaces and a café.

The Kunststad ("art city"), for instance, houses hundreds of artists, architects and designers; shipping containers have become student homes; and a former shipyard crane is now a hotel.

While the dwellings themselves vary hugely in both aesthetics and function, what's common to the entire area is the prevalence of street art: murals climb entire walls; tags abound; most walls have been decorated in one way or another (the Anne Frank mural on Straat's front is now a well-established landmark, visible from far across the water); kids come from far and wide to find the wall/canvases where they can freely learn their craft without being moved along by the authorities.

The area reminds me a bit of Hackney Wick about 15 years ago. The industrial past is celebrated rather than hidden: big warehouses many might deem eyesores, but embellished and enlivened by the creative types who first venture into these former urban wastelands and, inadvertently, lay the ground for their gentrification (the high-rises and corporate tenants now arriving on NDSM wharf suggest that process is well under way).

From shipbuilding yard to flea market to museum

The story of how Straat came to be is almost as colourful as its walls. It began as a flea market: the founder was asked to add some art to the venue, invited graffiti writers in, and was so struck by the public response that he decided to build a museum. He started building without the right permits. "I'd rather ask for forgiveness than full permission," is a line that Straat educator Alex Pope says sums up the approach.

Straat opened in 2020 – the worst possible timing thanks to Covid – and the founder's three daughters even remortgaged their houses to keep the venture afloat, a testament to their belief in the museum and the good it does.

It's hard to describe just how overwhelming, awe-inspiringly vast Straat Museum itself is: so much so that you barely notice a literal truck suspended from the ceiling, such is the way it's dwarfed by the scale of the walls around it.

ART IS NOT A CRIME Henry Chalfant B Boy op | on Broadway & 96th St Henry Chalfant

Straat's space is divided into four quadrants, taking in post-graffiti and abstraction, socially engaged work, and cultural heritage and identity. It houses a collection of roughly 280 works, more than 180 of them on show at any time, spanning canvas and special spray-friendly polyester, installations, video, sculpture and fibreglass.

Among the artists showing work there are Shepard Fairey (of Obama Hope poster fame); Shamsia Hassani, whose piece Amina speaks to the struggles and hopes of women in Afghanistan; Gary Stranger, whose typographic pieces are more than a little reminiscent of Lance Wyman's gorgeous Mexico Olympics graphics; and Dutch artist Nina Valkhoff, whose beautiful animal imagery weaves political messaging and a smidgen of surrealism into what's indisputably very, very cute.

The variation between works – in terms of medium, message, aesthetic, geographical background, artist age and pretty much everything else – is vast. Classical Greco-Roman iconography eventually leads to a very powerful piece formed of a Beirut shutter scarred by real bullet and shrapnel damage, which invites visitors to leave messages for people living through conflict, for instance.

The connection between Amsterdam's street art heritage and that of New York might not be all that well-known, but it is strong and storied.

According to Straat director Marion Wolff, when graffiti landed in the Netherlands, the country was in a political moment: housing shortages, riots, a city not unlike the New York that had arguably birthed the art form a decade earlier. The places with the strongest ties to street art, she reckons, are united by political and social discontent: neglected spaces become canvases, then come the cafés, the galleries, the rising rents, and eventually the commissions for slick developments.

Joker en Shoe voor een USA piece | Joker and Shoe in front of a USA piece, Amsterdam, 1985 Henry Chalfant K Slay in East Harlem Henry Chalfant

Rinse and repeat as history itself repeats; copy and paste from New York to Amsterdam to London to Berlin, and so on. "It all comes full circle," she says, "and that's the beauty of graffiti and street art."

Henry Chalfant: Art is Not a Crime

Street art's foundations in socioeconomic hardship are the palpable throughline of Straat's current exhibition, Art Is Not a Crime, a survey of the photography of Henry Chalfant. Now 86, Chalfant is a sculptor-turned-photographer who spent the late 1970s and early 80s documenting New York's subway graffiti, and who helped bring it to Europe through the book Subway Art, co-authored with Martha Cooper and first published in 1984, and as co-producer of the 1983 film Style Wars.

Photography and documentation matter a hell of a lot for graffiti and street art – more than pretty much any other art form – for the simple fact of their inherent fleetingness.

The star of the show here is Chalfant's extensive documentation of graffiti on New York trains – at Straat, the images are presented life/train size, on metallic paper, with a huge backlit screen showing cars "running" into the station. You can even watch them from New York subway benches, as the booming train track sound effects rattle around you.

Speelterrein | Playground, Amsterdam, 1985 Henry Chalfant

Chalfant photographed around 550 trains, with each car shot in four or five frames during the 30-second-or-so window of time in which a train sat at the platform. These were then spliced and glued together by hand.

The burning Bronx, and building trust

The backdrop is crucial. This was a city that was financially bankrupt and arguably morally too: dirty cops, drugs, the (literally) burnt-out South Bronx. Chalfant shot some incredible images that sum all this up: kids gleefully jumping off an old refrigerator onto rusty mattress bedsprings to perfect their gymnastics.

"All these art forms emerge outside of official chambers," he says. Hip-hop and graffiti grew up side by side with breaking and DJing, and Chalfant played his part: when asked by a gallery to do a performance piece, he invited pioneering breakdancers Crazy Legs and Frosty Freeze to the studio and gave them the stage.

What makes the pictures so strong, though, is the trust behind them. Chalfant is the first to admit he was hardly an obvious ally to the writers and dancers whose work is central to his own. "The profile they saw was that of a middle-aged white man, exactly like most of the cops," he says. "I looked like a cop, so people stayed away."

Chalfant grew up in privileged circumstances and had never had to defend himself. It took one kid, curious that this man seemed uninterested in him, to come over and ask what he was doing. Chalfant always made sure to have some of his snaps to hand – he showed them to the kid, who instantly recognised his cousin's piece in one of the images.

Writers Scene Henry Chalfant

From this interaction, Chalfant learned of a very specific site that no one outside of the scene could possibly have known of without actually working not only to earn their trust but also to be liked by those kids involved in it: a bench at 149th Street and the Grand Concourse, where everyone hung out after school. He went that afternoon and later invited the writers to his studio to see their work in albums, which gave them something to take to galleries and publishers.

Street art as a mirror, not a trend

The exhibition is a triumph, and not an easy one in a building like Straat which spans 8,000 square metres, 2,000 of which are given over to Chalfant's show. Presenting photographs of graffiti-laden trains as big as trains themselves feels like exactly the right response.

And if ever there was a good time to be showing work like this, it's now: a timeline on display in Straat itself traces the origins of graffiti back to student protests – and look at what's happening in Paris, and around the world right now.

The beauty of street art – no matter its setting, its more recent position as an art market commodity, its coffee table books and repurposing as nothing but a 'cool' Instagram background and icks; its format or function or medium or message – is that it's born of disillusionment, of trying to say something, of giving voice in the most public of public settings to those people who don't usually get to be heard.

With Halloween almost upon us, we've picked a Dear Boom missive that reads like the opening of a horror film.

A creative writes: "I've been working with a client for nearly two years. Every job starts as 'something really simple'. The brief changes halfway through. Finished projects come back from the dead. And somehow, I always end up agreeing to one more thing. I've been calling it a learning experience. But I think I've had enough now."

"The good news is, I'm ready to leave. But then I try to write the email. I've softened the latest draft so much it now sounds like I'm not trying to move away at all. I want to leave professionally, wish them well and experience the enormous pleasure of never receiving another 'quick question' from them on a Sunday evening, ever again."

So how do you make a clean exit when you're very good at saying yes and bad at saying goodbye? We put the question to our community on Instagram and The Studio. A couple of you suggested calling in an exorcist (Keiron Lewis helpfully notes you'll need it authorised by a bishop), but most had rather more practical advice...

1. Goodbye or renegotiation?

A few of you asked whether a break-up is actually necessary. Designer Nicholas Robb suggested explaining the problem in plain language and proposing a fix, such as fixed briefs or higher fees, arguing that you have nothing to lose. Illustrator and animator Csaba Klement suggested updating your contract so that changes cost money: "If they won't sign, your exit is clearly on them."

These sound like good options in some situations. But we'll believe our correspondent when they say they've firmly decided to leave. After all, if you'd be secretly disappointed when they said yes, any negotiation won't be a win, but a potential own goal.

2. Check your contract

As illustrator Charlotte Goodman-Brooks points out, when you break up with a client, check your contract first. If there's a notice period, honour it.

If there's no contract, though, creative director and consultant Paul Leon suggests you owe nothing beyond finishing what you agreed to. "I'd do my last project and walk away," he says. "You don't have to explain anything; you don't have to metaphorically pour petrol on the bridge and set it on fire, unless you really want to make a point.

3. Explain, but don't litigate

The biggest split in our discussions was over whether to give reasons at all. Designer Matthew Gallagher argues you don't owe anyone an explanation. "Merely let them know that your schedule is booked and you won't be able to take any more work from them," he recommends. "It gives them a heads-up, and you can move on by being gracious and without having to burn any bridges."

Photo retoucher and creative artworker Sandrine Bascouert, however, takes the view that the truth can be one last service to a client who's been surrounded by yes-people and has no idea they're a nightmare. "You don't have to be harsh or rude," she says. "Just tell it like it is, keep it short and make it clear there's no coming back."

Illustrator Lucila Solier Feiguin agrees. "You should probably give them the reasons, at least broadly speaking ('due to X/Y/Z the situation has escalated beyond the agreed budget and contract multiple times, which has made me take a step backwards to reassess')," she says. "Try focusing on the facts and what kind of consequences those had."

4. Writing the exit email

Now for what some might consider the biggest challenge: emailing the client. Designer Jessica Rebelo knows exactly where our correspondent is coming from. "I'm a people pleaser and have been there, accepting a lot of work, sometimes for very little money," she admits. "It's tricky!"

Lucila's tip is to put yourself in the shoes of "the boss" when you write the email, and allow yourself to state your boundaries. Then, crucially, don't undo them in the sign-off. "Don't add anything like 'but we could work together again', or you'll be basically telling them that your boundaries mean nothing," she warns.

A good exit email needs to tick three boxes. The client needs to clearly understand you won't be taking on further work. You need to define an end point: when you've caught up with the work you owe them, and you're done. And finally, a sincere good wish—but one that acts as a full stop, rather than a signal for further chat.

Madalena Studio did exactly this last year. "We said that our business focus was moving in a different direction and, whilst we had personally enjoyed and greatly appreciated working with them and their team, that they might be better finding a partner who was more aligned with their needs," the studio explains. "We politely gave them a four-week notice period, wished them well and sent their files over at the end."

Hand-painting illustrator Lee Gruber took a similar approach when ending a retainer. "I gave a month's notice, kept scope firm, and offered a handover with a clear end date," he says. "I even offered alternatives to fill my role."

5. The happy ending

Here's the part our correspondent will enjoy most. After all that agonising, how did Madalena Studio's client of four years respond? Quite simply: "I am aligned on this."

It often goes like this. You spend all that time pent up, dreading your reaction, and then nothing happens. It's anti-climactic for sure. But on the plus side, it leaves you to get on with your life, and that has to be a good thing.

The final part of this horror story? Make sure the sequel never gets made. As Paul stresses, solid contracts, terms, and work schedules protect you next time. Designer and founder Brian Winkeler goes further, telling every new client his agency has two set-in-stone rules. "We don't work with jerks," he says. "Anyone who can say 'No' must be part of the process." Do that, and your next horror story might never make it past the trailer.

Royal portrait painter swaps palaces for a Barnsley chippy, a Catford corner shop and a pub run by 'Slugger'

In Coca-Cola's latest campaign, four independent business owners get something usually reserved for kings and queens: an oil-on-canvas portrait by artists who've sat opposite the late Queen, King Charles and Princess Anne.

Before the camera came along, only the very rich could afford to have themselves immortalised in oils. I'm talking royalty, aristocrats, and the odd wealthy merchant; their portraits often hang in prestigious galleries or National Trust properties. Their noble gazes in gilt frames suggest a life of privilege, while everyone else went undocumented.

Even today, a formal portrait remains a status symbol. So it's quite unusual to find a portrait of the person who serves you chips on a Saturday night. But that's what's happened in a new campaign by Coca-Cola. The drinks brand has commissioned four of Britain's finest portrait artists, each of whom has painted an official portrait of a member of the Royal Family, to paint a fish-and-chip shop owner, a café proprietor, a pub landlord and a corner shop owner. The finished works will be on show at London's Saatchi Gallery from today until 1 November, and admission is free.

It's the latest chapter in Coca-Cola's 'Localness' platform, which celebrates the people behind the pubs, shops and cafés where its drinks are served. It follows last year's documentary and photo series, The Bosses, and Counter Cultures earlier in 2026. This time around, the campaign borrows the visual language of official portraiture. And we love it. Because why shouldn't a Barnsley chippy get the same gravitas as Buckingham Palace?

Pindy Kaur by Nicky Philipps Simon Dafforn-Jones by Jamie Coreth

Everyone else seems to agree. New research commissioned by Coca-Cola Great Britain found that 79% of people think independent businesses are under-appreciated, while 80% want to see them celebrated more often. "Independent business owners create the places where local life happens, yet their contribution often goes unrecognised," says Rhona Stephen, senior director of communications for Coca-Cola Great Britain. "With Local Royalty, we wanted to celebrate them in a way that felt deliberately unexpected and enduring. By borrowing the visual language of official portraiture and placing these owners at its centre, the campaign gives their everyday impact the status it deserves."

So who got their portrait done? First up is Pindy Kaur, who took over a struggling Ocean Fish Bar in Barnsley in the 1990s. More than 25 years on, she and her family have turned it into a much-loved takeaway, known for its regulars and its family-run spirit. Pindy is a devoted admirer of the late Queen Elizabeth II, and she was painted by Nicky Philipps, who painted the Queen not once but twice.

Gino Verecchia by Gareth Reid Kaual Patel by Nick Bashall

Gino Verecchia's University Café in Glasgow was opened by his grandfather, Pasquale, in 1918, and it's been in the family for three generations. Wow. It blends Italian heritage (including a 100-year-old gelato recipe) with Scottish favourites, and it's a true Glasgow institution. It even once served Anthony Bourdain battered haggis. Gareth Reid, portrait artist to King Charles III when he was Prince of Wales, painted Gino.

Down in Dittisham, South Devon, meanwhile, The Ferry Boat Inn sits on the banks of the River Dart. Locals know it as the FBI. Brilliant. Landlord Simon Dafforn-Jones, or "Slugger" to the locals, has run it for almost 20 years, following in his dad's footsteps. He gives local teens their first jobs and champions produce from across the region, so the pub naturally sits at the heart of village life. He was painted by Jamie Coreth, who created the first official portrait of the Prince and Princess of Wales.

And finally, Kaual Patel's father bought Torridon Convenience Store in Catford, South East London, in 1984, and Kaual took over in 2000. Over the past two decades, he has made it a real community hub, stocking local producers alongside the bigger household names. His mantra is lovely: "If you're a local producer, there's a space on my shelf for you." Kaual was painted by Nick Bashall, portrait painter to Princess Anne.

Kaual Patel by Nick Bashall Gino Verecchia by Gareth Reid Pindy Kaur by Nicky Philipps Simon Dafforn-Jones by Jamie Coreth

There you have it. All worthy contenders. All beautifully depicted. Alongside their finished portraits, the Saatchi show includes behind-the-scenes footage from the sittings. It shows how each artist got to know their sitter and turned their character and history into a formal portrait.

Each owner also donates to a local charity of their choice and keeps the portrait to hang in their home or business.

MHP Group created and delivered the campaign across MHP, Mischief and Studio La Plage. Find out more on Coca-Cola's website. So next time you're enjoying a pint or picking up a chippy tea, take a moment to appreciate the people behind the counter. You might be in the presence of royalty.

Span's identity for Illinois Department of Human Rights turns a common piece of punctuation into a rallying cry

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Inspired by the posters and placards of past civil rights movements, the rebrand makes people's rights easier to see and use.

Since 1980, the Illinois Department of Human Rights (IDHR) has investigated discrimination in employment, housing, financial institutions, and public spaces based on race, sex, disability, religion, national origin, and age.

The organisation has now partnered with Chicago design studio Span on a people-centred rebrand. The aim: reinforce its commitment to protecting human rights while making those rights easier to see, understand, and use.

While IDHR aims to protect all people across Illinois, the department feared citizens could no longer see that purpose, as trust in government and public institutions has grown more fragile.

"There was a concern that the organisation was seen as rigid and bureaucratic, with its important work less visible," says Nick Adam, associate partner and design director at Span. "IDHR needed to remain neutral and fair in its work without becoming neutral about its purpose.

"And while they understood that a new logo couldn't mend an institutional divide, they knew that they could better protect people's rights by raising awareness; changing how IDHR speaks, organises information and shows up in public. After all, a right people cannot see is harder to use."

Focus on clarity

To meet this challenge, the new identity focuses on clarity to help users understand their options and feel supported.

For inspiration, the team returned to the symbols of past civil rights efforts: posters, placards, banners, and declarations designed to be seen, understood, and remembered.

The main typeface, HEX Franklin, is bold and forceful, conveying confidence, credibility and trust.

In the logo, the words "Human Rights" are emphasised to keep the department's mission front and centre, with "Illinois Department of" serving as both a descriptor and a connector.

Positioned between the two large lines of "Human Rights", it creates the visual impression of an equal sign: a simple but meaningful gesture towards fairness, reciprocity and balance under the law.

A sign of equality

"The equal sign gave us a way to make the idea of human rights visible before a single word is read," says Adam. "Equality belongs to everyone."

He adds that the lines echo both flag stripes and the graphic language of designers like Chicago's Lester Beall, celebrated for his work for the Rural Electrification Administration.

Social and rapid-response communications have been built as a deployment kit, with established ways to handle announcements, photography, rights information, events and urgent messages.

Public events required another ready-to-use system of banners, flags, step-and-repeats, table coverings, signage, presentations and printed materials.

This has helped IDHR put rights information directly into people's hands at Juneteenth events and the Illinois State Fair. At Chicago's Pride Parade, it allowed the department to create and distribute stickers, buttons, apparel and other materials to the crowd.

Span has a longstanding relationship with IDHR, having previously helped launch the statewide support service Help Stop Hate. And Adam stresses that this project has been built for the long haul, dubbing it: "Conviction without propaganda. That feels especially important in design right now. And as Illinois locals, we can see and feel the impact of our own work in real time."

IDHR director Jim Bennett is delighted with the result. "Our goal was to create a brand that people could recognise, connect with and immediately know our agency exists to protect their rights," he says. "Every Illinoisan should know they are protected under state law, regardless of who they are, who they love, or where they come from."

UX Disciplines: Glossary

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Summary: Definitions of fields related to user experience, including UX design, UI design, visual design, interaction design, service design, UX research, and DesignOps


People often use terms such as UX , UI , CX , and visual design interchangeably, even though they refer to different fields of practice. This glossary focuses primarily on what each kind of work involves. Some of these terms (such as user experience and customer experience ) also have other common meanings. For example, user experience can refer to the field of user experience or to the experience of users interacting with a particular product, while information architecture can refer to the field of information architecture or the content organization on a specific site. Where useful, we note these additional meanings.

Job titles in our field are not standardized. In our UX-careers survey , respondents reported 134 unique job titles related to UX . As a result, the boundaries described here are the typical ones; in any given organization, one person may do the work of several of these areas, and the same title may mean different things.

Some fields in this glossary (e.g., market research) are not traditionally part of UX but are adjacent to it and often confused with it; others (e.g., UX research) are subfields of UX.
